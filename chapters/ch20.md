As Maureen made her way down the lower corridors, she noticed that while the black-robed seeds continued to ignore each other as before, there were more furtive looks, and hands hovered near their belts. Up on deck, hundreds of the seeds stood apart in an uneasy silence. Here and there, trios of black-armoured knights stood in the open, their plated feet planted firmly on the timber planks.

Evans had given instructions for obtaining food. A seed could approach a trio and stand ten feet in front of them for thirty breaths. The knights would then give a portion of food. Only one portion of food was available per trio per day, and once the food ran out, no more would be provided until the next day.

[10 ft]

Tally highlighted a line on the planks ten feet in front of each trio of knights. There are 81 knights here. [Radiation: none. No data: men?]

“They’ve got twenty-seven groups,” Richard whispered to her.

Maureen nodded, allowing him his discovery. I saw it first, she thought.

Tally sniffed.

So whose breaths do I count? Maureen asked silently.

No one defined what a breath is. I’ll use your breathing pattern set at Silver Light and pad the number a little bit.

A single stroke boomed out from the great brass bell suspended high above on a timber frame. The sound echoed through the ship’s hull like a peal of thunder, rolling through the corridors. For a moment, no one moved, then a single figure broke and ran for the knights. In seconds, the entire deck surged into motion, a tide of shouting figures scrambling for position. Elbows jostled, and the stink of unwashed, hungry bodies rose around her. The tarred planks pulled at her boots as the deck lifted beneath the great tortoise’s breath.

Maureen headed towards the nearest trio of black-armoured knights, taking their line. A seed grabbed her arm, and she twisted her wrist to break his grip before slamming her hands against two others, sending them crashing down to the deck. Her forearms bunched as the cords stood out on her skin.

> 18… 19…

the counter in her eye said.

> Behind-left, footsteps. Two, by sound.

Tally told her.

Fists only, Maureen. Keep the Cross Sword in its sheath. You don’t need to risk the edge touching them and poisoning them with the blue one. Don’t show any more strength than you have to.

I know, she told Tally silently.

At thirty, according to Tally’s count, the middle knight held out a black cloth bag. She snatched it from his hand and bolted for another trio.

The bag was made from rough black cloth with the neck tied shut. It was heavier than it looked and smelled faintly of roast fat. At the second trio of knights, she found four wizard seeds standing on the line, one holding the bag while three others stood guard around him.

> Three guards. Middle one’s the leader: the others look to him for orders.

Tally informed her.

Maureen drew the Cross Sword, stepped forward and used the flat of the blade to smash the middle guard down to the deck. The other two seeds fell back, leaving the bag-holder frozen in place, clutching the bag tightly.

Maureen levelled the Cross Sword at him. “You know what to do, I take it?”

Tally alerted her to the fact that the seed’s grip on the bag had loosened, and he managed to force a smile as he tossed the bag over. As soon as it left his hands, he bolted away. At the third trio, Maureen drove off several seeds with the flat of the Cross Sword, but before she could grab the bag, the great brass bell rang out again. The food was gone, and it was time to run for her cabin.

* * *

She barred the door, and started spinning a web. The rope and grappling claw were her first line of defence, followed by the chains attached to the bunk, the storm chain she’d pried off an empty cabin and the hooks bent from the irons at the foot of the bunks. Tally provided a model of her stride length and the arc the door swung through, then calculated and displayed the optimum heights for trip wires in a pale blue translucent line. She moved one of the wires up a few centimetres, just in case Richard needed to get in. Tally dutifully logged his height.

There was rust on her palms from the iron chains, and the links were cold against her skin, but now anyone who forced their way into her cabin would end up maimed or killed.

She sat on the bunk, and opened both bags. The smell of pine-nut jam, salted beef jerky, goose liver roasted in its own fat, crusty white bread and lamb meatballs filled the air. The two bags had different contents. Tally logged each item as Maureen checked it against her road food.

> Shelf life: Beef Jerky 9+ days, Goose Liver 1 day, Lamb Meatballs 1 day.

"Damn it!" Maureen thumped the plank beside her with her fist. So much for stockpiling. Evans had known. He’d built this game around the assumption that she would try to build up a store of food, and he’d made sure that she couldn’t.

Evans read the same tables I did, Tally said.

Well, thanks, that’s a big help.

She drew a sharp breath, feeling the fat from the roast goose liver on her fingers. She put the white bread to one side. It wouldn’t keep long enough. As she sorted the food into two piles, storable and perishable, Tally read out the totals.

> Two standard meals per bag. Two days total.

Not enough.

She stashed the storable food under the bunk, making sure it was dry. Then she ate, performed her Spirit Rune meditation and Silver Light, strung the bell cord across the doorway, armed the sleeve crossbow and went to sleep. The wooden hull creaked in sympathy with the great tortoise’s slow breathing. Twice during the night, she heard footfalls pass her door. They stopped briefly both times, then continued on their way.

* * *

By the second day, after a full day's hunger, several seeds were killed outright when the bell rang. Anyone holding food was instantly overwhelmed by starving men and women.

One seed died for a single lamb meatball. The little sphere of spiced meat rolled away across the tarred planks.

By the fourth day most of the seeds were gaunt, their eyes green with hunger. They had the intense fixed stare of wolves.

Maureen could see her own healthy face reflected in their envious stares. Few looked as she did.

No one was able to hold off a large team by themselves, so after the second day Maureen formed her own team. She wanted Richard and Aaron with her, but Richard insisted on having Lucy with them, and Lucy refused to leave Alice's side.

Maureen's jaw set. It was obvious to everyone that Richard's voice rose half a tone whenever he spoke to Lucy, and Maureen let it ride.

> Two more mouths to feed. Two more to guard.

> Two more pairs of eyes. Alice is steady with a blade.

Which side are you on? she asked it silently.

Yours. That is why I list both columns.

* * *

The open deck was not the same on the fifth day. The overcast sky darkened everything, and the wind was up, snapping the rigging with a dry crackle. The brass bell swung quietly above their heads, its song stilled. The old blood staining the tarred planks had darkened, the rusty hue almost matching the black pitch. Before the bell rang, Richard leaned in close to Maureen. "This round looks bad," he whispered.

Maureen patted his shoulder in assent. "Aaron, Lucy, Alice, stay with Richard."

Lucy nodded mutely. Her flaxen hair fell across her face as she bowed her head. Gentle Alice gave a short nod as well, her face serene. Aaron's smile did not reach the other half of his face. His thumb worked at the hem of his sleeve, plucking at the threads.

The bell rang, and this scramble was even more brutal than before. When they regrouped, there were four black bags at their feet. There was fresh blood on the planks, and both Aaron and Alice bore a superficial cut on their shoulders. "It's nothing," each insisted when Maureen glanced at them with concern.

Aaron pulled out a loaf of white bread from one of the bags. "At least it was worth the trouble," he said, breaking off a piece. Someone darted in and snatched most of the bread from his hands.

Carey.

Maureen recognized the freckled seed from Skran immediately. He crammed the bread into his mouth, crumbs falling to the deck from his chin. "It's just bread," he said around a mouthful. "Just bread."

Carey's fingers are bare, Tally noted to Maureen. All his rings are gone, including the stone from the treasury of Skran. He has traded everything for food. He does not wear a sword, nor does he stand with a knight's stance.

Maureen nodded fractionally and slowly drew the Cross Sword. "I am Carey," the seed said, "of grade seven high aptitude. We come from the same place, the same squad."

Aptitude counted before we boarded this ship, Maureen told Tally silently.

Strength is all that matters on this deck, it replied. Carey has none.

"The bread was yours," Maureen said aloud to Aaron. "The decision is yours."

"Where's Ellena?" Aaron asked, scowling at the seed.

Carey interrupted, swallowing another wad of bread. "After we boarded, she went off with some other seed, a black-robe." He spat. "Slut. You're low-aptitude trash anyway."

Tally flagged a tremor in Aaron. His head bowed and the veins stood out on the hand that gripped his sword.

"Listen," Carey said, "you can do better than her. I remember Ellena — she wasn't anything special. Forget her, Aaron. Once you become a wizard, women will be plentiful. Follow me, and this little bit of bread won't matter."

"Enough, Carey," Maureen snapped, "what will you do, Aaron?" She glanced up at the brass bell above, silent now, then back at Aaron.

Carey laughed. "Nothing, he's a worm. Let it go, Aaron."

Maureen's hand tightened on the hilt of her sword, then relaxed.

[Aaron: tremor ceased. Grip steady.] Tally told her.

She understood what that meant.

Do you wish to intervene? Tally asked quietly.

No, she told it silently.

Logged, replied Tally.

Aaron looked up at her, and there was a gentle smile on his face. "You're right, Carey," he said. "It'll be fine."

Carey laughed and turned to leave.

Aaron ran him through from behind, the blade punching out through Carey's chest. The deck fell silent, even the wind seemed to stop blowing for a moment. Carey stared down at the blade sticking out of his chest, the blood welling around it, his mouth working without sound.

Aaron stood close behind him, almost touching his back. "It'll be fine, Carey," he said, voice as soft as if he were whispering in Carey's ear. "Once you're dead, none of this will matter any more. Will it?" He pulled the Cross Sword out. Carey's body toppled heavily to the deck, blood running into the seams between the planks where tar sealed the wood. The torn ball of bread slipped from the seed's nerveless fist. She watched, not turning away.

* * *

Maureen sat on the bunk under the low hanging lamp, hands resting quietly with palms flat on her knees. The smell of the deck's blood still clung to her, even in the quiet of her cabin.

She divided up her share of the four bags, eating the perishables while putting the jerky away where it would stay dry. Then she waited, silently willing Tally to tell her that she was right.

I can't do that, Maureen, Tally answered honestly. I have no data on what 'right' is. Only on what happens.

What do you have data on?

Aaron's hand was steady when he did it and yours still is, Tally told her.

Maureen stared at her hand for a long moment, letting the lamplight play across it. That was enough. She lay down on the bunk beside the bell cord.
