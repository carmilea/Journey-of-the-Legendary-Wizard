The posting parties were mustering on the broad stone apron where the road from the freight-lift yard met the road to the gate. Maureen sat perched on the box of her own black-lacquered carriage. The two horn-plated beasts before her blew great clouds of steam into the chill morning air. She held the reins loosely in her hands, and they were quite steady. One hand, though, had a tendency to wander down to her coat. The long, dark-gold box rested against her ribs, sending a cold spot through the cloth that was more noticeable now than it had been before. It was heavier than it looked. Adrian’s ‘Come back safe’ lay in her chest like a stone she’d swallowed.

*You’ve checked the box three times since we got off the lift,* Tally pointed out.

*I know,* she thought back silently. *It’s still there.*

*So am I,* Tally said. *And I’m the more important entry.*

Above the gate, the living statue known as Hugh leaned down from its post, swinging its great, lizard-shaped head over the little column. It made its worst face, eyes bulging from their sockets, scarlet tongue lolling obscenely out to its full length. A gob of spit hit the black flagstones with a loud hiss, beads of moisture dancing away from the impact point.

No one on the apron below even flinched. Daniel looked up at Hugh and grinned. Hasen kept his eyes firmly fixed on Lise. Lise inspected a glove. The six knights of their escort didn’t even raise their visors to get a look. After two years of dealing with the statue’s pranks, Maureen merely glanced back up at it.

[Hugh: face 7 of 7 on file. Repeat.]

*I think Hugh is a little sad to see us go,* Maureen thought.

Tally had no data on that.

*But . . . it did it slower this time.*

Hugh drew its head back up onto its perch, its tongue sliding back into its mouth with a wet sound. “A-apprentices,” the statue grumbled, looking down at them. “G-getting worse. Every year, w-worse.”

Tally dutifully added the line to Hugh’s folder before logging the statue as the final gatekeeper on file to bid them farewell.

The column rolled forward. The supply wagon was flanked by the six knights, Lise drove her tiny cart, loaded down with enough belongings to sink a small ship. And Maureen rolled out in her carriage, driving herself. Hasen and Daniel rode beside the supply wagon.

The last of the knotted black trunks thinned out, then fell away completely, giving way all at once to the green forest that covered the hills. One moment they were rolling past gnarled, knotted black trees, the next they were in green hill forest, passing beneath the boughs of oaks and elms, beeches and poplars. Wet green sunlight sifted through the leaves, and the sharp tang of pine resin cut through the cold incense that still seemed to cling to her robes. It was like driving from one world into another.

Somewhere in the green a bird sang. *First birdsong since yellow bird in realm cave. Singing bird logged,* Tally said.

*Log it as a bird,* Maureen thought back silently.

*Already did,* Tally replied.

The sun shone in earnest now, and the knights’ armour began to tick as the steel warmed up. The horses’ hot-iron scent rose from their black-scaled flanks, mingling with the smell of pine resin in the air.

Tally ran its blue route ribbon far ahead of the horses’ ears, south and west. [Panso Mountains, 92%] hovered on the horizon, at the end of the ribbon.

*I’ll worry about the eight when we get there,* Tally said.

*You already said that,* Maureen thought.

*Some things bear repeating.*

The road stretched before them for many days. At dusk on the first evening the party pulled off the road into a clearing and made camp.

* * *

Maureen sat beside the fire, a green stick in her hands slowly rotating a skinned rabbit over the flames. The supply wagon had enough food for everyone. It wasn’t necessary to cook for the group, but it was something she’d never done, cooking over an open flame with her own two hands.

The fat bubbled on the rabbit meat, then beaded up and dropped onto the coals. It hissed and popped. The smoke stung Maureen’s eyes, but it smelled like nothing in the slum or like anything in the Dark Forest dining hall.

Tally timed her turns and offered a doneness estimate, based solely on colour since it didn’t have a thermometer. Maureen overruled it and left the stick still. One side of the rabbit blackened, and Tally noted that it was logging the event as cooking.

Lise walked over and stood above Maureen, her shadow falling across the fire. She tilted her chin up so she could look down at both Maureen and the rabbit. “I’ve brought rather a lot,” Lise said. “My cart’s crammed. Your carriage must be half empty. Apprentices like you, without any family, never really have much of anything. Take your things out and let mine go in.” She paused to bite her lower lip. “Call it a favour?”

Tally flagged the bitten lip as a practised gesture, marking it as the second instance since this morning when Lise had used it on the knights. *The request is a test of social rank, not an offer of a favour,* Tally noted. *I know,* Maureen thought silently back to Tally.

*Yes,* Tally replied, *I know you know. I’m just keeping track.*

Maureen didn’t raise her eyes from the turning rabbit. “No,” she said evenly. “That’s a favour I don’t care to do.”

Maureen’s jaw clenched once and then relaxed. Underneath her carriage seat were thirty-four grenades, all packed in padded crates, along with every other possession she owned in the world. The idea of hauling everything out just to make room for Lise’s hatboxes was almost amusing. The rabbit kept on turning over the fire.

Lise’s face grew hard, and the corners of her mouth turned white.

“Maureen!” Hasen came striding across the clearing, his voice booming before he’d crossed half the distance between them.

A red threat mark bloomed over his head. Tally analysed his stride. *Angry, not fast.*

Hasen’s eyes flicked to Lise first. Only then did he turn to look at Maureen.

Maureen read the rest on her own. This was a man who’d seen the door of a wizard family swing open an inch. His chest puffed out, his posture falling into what he obviously imagined was the gallant stance, ready to be seen by the girl. The speech was already forming in his mouth.

Before a word could leave his lips, Maureen raised her free hand.

“Simons-Sado . . .” she began, breathing deeply. “Amra-Keis!”

Tally painted two bars of syllables across Maureen’s vision, Simons-Sado on the beat, Amra-Keis on the off-beat. The lines intersected each other like fingers, a complex pattern of sound, and a timing call appeared over each intersection.

*Now. Now.*

And her mouth and hands moved to match the commands, weaving the chants together.

In front of her, at arm’s length, a ball of fire and a ball of acid appeared. They burned side by side, the deep-red fireball and the swirling orb of green. Each was about the size of her fist, and the fireball roared to life while the acid slowly expanded into a larger sphere. White smoke poured from both.

The fireball’s heat washed across her cheek. The acid’s acrid smell caught in her nose and throat. The two orbs burned so brightly that they illuminated Hasen’s face from both sides. The red and the green painted him, one cheek bright and hot, the other sickly and burning with a different fire. Behind her, the fat from the rabbit continued to drip into the fire with its regular hiss.

[Mana 11.2 → 9.7. Holding.]

*Both models are stable. Can continue indefinitely. If you wish.*

The colour drained from Hasen’s face. He took an involuntary step back. “S-split chant?” he asked. His voice sounded thin. “You can . . . Maureen, that’s . . . only the real top of the Third-Class can do that.”

“Remember this,” Maureen said as she looked at him over the two points of light. “From now on, don’t bother me.”

Over at the knights’ fire, the low conversation had ceased. One of them sat with his spoon halfway to his mouth, watching.

*It costs you nothing extra to let those two spells go. It would cost you a great deal of pride to throw them. Mostly his.* Tally pointed out.

Maureen let the spells go. The fireball collapsed in upon itself with a last puff of heat, while the acid spell hissed away into steam.

Hasen remained frozen where he was, and Lise’s mouth had compressed into a thin, unhappy line.

“Hey. What’s all this, then?”

Daniel crunched an apple as he strolled up to join them. He looked from one face to the next, then grinned as if he had just figured out the entire situation with a single glance. “Didn’t you two know? Maureen here walked out of the secret realm alive. Fourteenth. Came back with the rewards and everything.” He shook his head and whistled. “Makes a man jealous.”

[Daniel (2).]

Tally identified him, recalling the first Daniel, Olit’s servant. A dead man. The one who had dusted Scentless Bug powder along her left sleeve as she entered Olit’s shop.

*He’s not the same Daniel. That Daniel is dead.*

*Yes, but this Daniel knows about your placing. They didn’t post that on the notice board, did they? How did he find out?*

The post-mates exchanged an embarrassed look.

Hasen took Lise by the elbow and steered her away from Maureen and back towards their own fire. The forest clearing settled down. Maureen finished off the rabbit, burnt side and all. It tasted smoky, but was better than she expected.

* * *

After that night though, Hasen made sure he kept out of her way. When they met at the water barrel, he flushed bright red, mumbled something incomprehensible, then hurried off to his fire with his cup half filled. Tally kept count of how many times he went out of his way to avoid her. Four times in two days. *Do you want a running total?* it asked. *No,* Maureen told it silently. *Fine, I’ll keep one anyway, then,* it said.

The days blurred into each other, as they travelled through the forest of green hills. Wet ruts sucked at the carriage wheels, the horses gave off a hot-iron smell, the wagon creaked ahead, and at the end of each day, a curl of blue resin smoke rose from the cookfires. Lise on the other hand, began to take an interest. Across the fire from Maureen, perched on her own cart, or at the midday halts, Lise’s eyes were drawn again and again to Maureen. She studied the woman with a strange, intense look.

Tally logged the looks. *Not hostility,* it said. *Appraisal. The same look buyers gave the pens at Julo’s.*

*That’s an ugly comparison,* Maureen told it silently.

*Yes, but an accurate one.*

One afternoon, at a halt, Lise strolled across and stood beside the carriage box, smoothing down her skirt. “My family looks after its own, you know,” she said, her voice softer than before. “Someone with your gifts, sworn to us—”

Without being asked, Tally put the Walker terms quietly on her display.

150 years.

80%.

For a brief moment Maureen saw Lilith again, standing outside her door late at night, hooded in black and making the same kind of offer, walking away in tears. Her throat tightened.

“I’ve no interest in serving a wizard family,” Maureen said, cutting her off. It came out shorter than she had intended.

*Tally, take it down,* Maureen said silently.

*Taken down,* Tally answered. *Not deleted.*

Lise froze for a second, then a tremor passed through her slender body. She turned back to look at Maureen, giving her a long, deep look before turning on her heel and walking away without another word.

Maureen gathered up the reins again. Her hands were steady as she did so, but that didn’t stop her fingers finding the cold edge of the box in her coat pocket.

* * *

Daniel, meanwhile, had a habit of appearing at her elbow. On the road, at meals, even beside her carriage when they stopped to make camp. He would talk about anything and everything. The weather, the scenery, his post-mates, anything to fill the time. She replied, if at all, in a single word.

Each time he got too close for comfort, her hand would twitch down to her left sleeve. Tally noted the reflex from Olit’s shop, and Maureen quickly turned it into straightening out her cuff before anyone could see.

At the stream she knelt by the side, wet a cloth in the clear water and used it to wipe down Freya’s slender Cross Sword. The water was cold over the brown stones, running between them with a burbling song. Ferns grew thickly along the banks, and where the sunlight touched the water it sparkled. The black light crawled over the wet blade like oil on water, and Maureen’s knuckles began to grow numb under the chill.

“Want a secret, Maureen?” Daniel stood over her, blinking owlishly, one finger raised in the air. “My talent’s a bit strange, actually. I can find any rare wizard plants or materials within five kilometres of me.”

The cloth paused in its wiping.

Maureen looked up at him, squinting in the sunlight. “Five kilometres?”

[Claim: 5 km. Verification: none possible.]

Tally answered without elaboration, unable to test Daniel’s range. It also had no data on whether the claim was true or false.

Then Maureen smiled, her teeth flashing in the sunlight. “Lucky you,” she said. “That’s a talent worth envying.” She turned back to the blade, rubbing away some imaginary stain.

Tally noted that smile. *That was for him.*

*Yes,* Maureen agreed silently. *Yes it was.*

“Oh, it’s not much really,” he said, laughing as he crouched down beside her. His breath smelled of apple, and his grin was just as sunny as ever. “But there is a good one near here. A Bone-Eating Flower. And it’s close!”

Maureen nodded, looking back up at him with a serious expression.

That night they heard him from across the camp, his voice ringing out from the shadows.

“Maureen! Maureen, come and look!”

She followed the sound to where he stood under the lanterns, holding up a small white flower for her to see.

“Yes?” Maureen said, reaching out and taking it from him. The flower was small in her palm, but what struck her most was the coldness of it. It felt like she’d grabbed a lump of ice, and the tips of her fingers were coated with a faint white frost. She felt a shiver run up her arm before she could suppress it.

Tally matched the flower against Adrian’s plate, while simultaneously matching the cold against her sensory memory: [Bone-Eating Flower. Match: Adrian's plate.]

The flower was real. She barely needed Tally’s confirmation; years of working at Adrian’s bench had already given her the answer.

“It’s real,” she said, handing it back to Daniel.

“We went with him,” Hasen said, and Lise nodded beside him. “We saw where it was growing with our own eyes.”

Both of them were staring at Daniel with open admiration, and there were a couple knights behind them who had wandered over as well, the lantern light swinging over their faces as they looked down at the small flower. One of them whistled quietly.

Maureen nodded, then held up one of the lanterns and walked over to the spot where Daniel said he found the flower. She wanted to see it for herself.

She crouched down by a small hole in the dark bank. The cut stem was still beaded with cold sap, and there was a faint ring of frost around the hole that hadn’t yet melted back into the soil.

Tally overlaid an image of the hole on Maureen’s vision: fresh-cut sap still wet, soil crumb consistent with the bank. Consistent with a talent. Also consistent with someone who planted it this morning.

Maureen stood up and shook her head with a smile. “Truly a remarkable talent.”

Tally logged Daniel as friendly. Unconfirmed. Still.

* * *

Daniel kept finding things. A rare root here, a strange moss there. Soon, no one had any doubts about his talent. And anyone with any sense could tell how much such a talent might be worth. When they stopped to make camp each night, the apprentices crowded around him, offering him their best rations, a pull from their wineskin, or a laugh at his jokes. His loud laughter echoed across the whole camp.

Lise now directed all her warmth toward Daniel. In camp, it was whispered that the two of them had gone into the woods together and returned well after dark.

*Hearsay,* logged Tally.

Tally kept its own count of Daniel’s findings as well. It noted that each one had turned up within easy walking distance of their campsite.

Maureen kept to her carriage and her own small fire. Over the days she warmed up a degree. Not toward Daniel, Lise, or anyone else, but toward the fire and the road ahead.

Then they began to see ridges rising in the distance, grey-blue against the dark green slopes of the pines. The Panso Mountains lay ahead—their destination, where they’d be stationed.

Tally overlaid an image of what it expected to see on the horizon. The major ridges matched up perfectly, but one spur still didn’t match.

*We’ll find out soon enough what that eight percent is,* Maureen thought silently.
