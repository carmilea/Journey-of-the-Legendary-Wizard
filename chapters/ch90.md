Maureen stood half against a tree trunk, Jack’s gaunt face fixed in place. Smoke from Ash’s body lay among the ferns, and the scent of burnt hair mixed with the sweet smell of blossoms fallen to earth. Both rested at the back of her throat. Her hands were loose, motionless, one of them near the inner pocket where the stone beat against her ribs.

Tally provided a new line of text in the corner of her sight: [Mana: 1.9]

In its own warm voice it noted, *That comes out to a single fireball and a great deal of posture.*

*Posture is all I have. So help me hold it.* Maureen answered silently.

Ani took several steps forward, breaking away from the group. She was the oldest of the dark elves, and the silver tooling on her leathers was scratched from the run through the trees. She stared at Jack with a pale face lined with worry. “Lord Jack,” she said in Ancient Andas, choosing each word as if it might break, “will you trade with us?”

Maureen let the question hang in the air for one beat longer than was natural before replying in the same language. Her voice was a low rasp that rolled like two stones in the back of her throat. “Whether I trade or not is my business. You want me interested? Tell me what you’re carrying.”

[Back vowel.] [Low.] The rasp was creeping up in pitch, half a tone closer to her own voice. [Lower.]

Maureen forced the sound down into the throat Mimicry had carved for her.

Ani’s mouth thinned into a displeased line. She was a court attendant, accustomed to bargaining from Freya’s side. Now, she was asking permission from a stranger. Behind her, the others shifted their weight from foot to foot. Not one of them looked at the body in the ferns.

Ani drew a deep breath. “We have on our persons several Underworld goods,” she said. Each word was measured and slow, like a steward reciting an inventory to a creditor. “Stone-blue flowers, flame ox hooves, purple centipede blood.”

As each item was named, one of the elves reflexively touched the pack or belt pouch that held it. Maureen noted where each item was hidden. The leather creaked as they moved. Somewhere above them, a branch dripped onto the fern carpet.

[Underworld. New. No data.] Tally tagged each item as it was named. [Underworld. New. No data.] [Underworld. New. No data.]

*I’m opening new shelves,* it told her in its own voice. Greedy. Pleased.

*Quiet about the shelves, please.*

“Ghost-faced spider eggs. Black goat hearts. And . . .”

[Ghost-faced spider eggs. Minai material. Match: Adrian’s plate.]

In the same breath Tally spoke in Maureen’s head. *Your fingers twitched there, Jack. Don’t do that.*

*They didn’t,* she thought back.

*They did. They had the frame.*

Heat rose in her neck beneath the illusion, but that was not important right now. Two of the Totem’s four materials were now within reach, and on the same day. She kept her eyes fixed on a single fern frond that stirred near Ani’s boot, and let Jack’s face slip into an expression of bored patience.

“. . . along with several smaller items,” Ani continued. “A few roots and powders, all well aged. So, Lord Jack, what do you think of our wares?”

The worry had crept into her voice like mould through cheese.

Maureen nodded slowly, then allowed Jack’s thin lips to stretch into a smile. “Good. Very good. You’ve come well supplied.”

“Does this mean you will trade with us?” Ani asked.

Maureen nodded again, and the relief passed over Ani’s face like the sun peeking through the clouds. “Then tell me what you want, and I will see that you . . .”

As Maureen opened her mouth, Tally began to count. *There are seven of them. You’ll need seven cards. If they refuse to deal, you have one fireball.*

*They won’t refuse,* Maureen said silently.

*How do you know that?*

*Ash,* she answered.

Maureen’s voice cut across Ani’s words, flat and rasping like a rusted nail drawn over a slate. Her eyes moved from one elf to the next, as if counting coins. “I will take all the items you have named. And there are seven of you.” Her eyes flicked back to Ani. “That is seven low-grade stone cards. A thousand stones a head.”

The young elf’s hand dropped to his hilt. “Insolent!” he shouted. The other elves erupted in a storm of outrage, their voices bouncing off the old trunks.

Maureen Olsen did not move. Her left hand rested on her thigh, well away from Freya’s sword, deliberately. The bark at her back was cold and damp through the cloak.

The shouting died away quickly in the thick, sweet air, as though the forest swallowed the sounds as soon as they were spoken.

Ani raised one hand without turning, and the elves fell silent. Her face had lost some colour. “Your terms are cruel, Lord Jack.”

“Cruel?” Maureen laughed, a dry sound, like stones rattling in an empty sack. “You chased me through half this forest for one stone. A thing that people die chasing doesn’t come cheap.”

She paused, then continued more softly. “And Ani... you’re not just buying back a stone. You’re buying the walk home.”

One by one, the dark elves’ eyes went down to the smouldering ferns where Ash lay. A curl of smoke still rose from the wreckage that had once been a dark elf. No one spoke, and the silence spread from face to face until the clearing was hushed.

Tally was keeping count of something else.

[Ani: glances at the sword 4.]

Freya’s slender blade hung from Maureen’s hip. That was why Maureen kept her hand away from the hilt. The sword was not part of the deal.

Ani’s face changed again, emotions flickering across her finely made features. Pride. Grief. Arithmetic.

Maureen waited patiently. She let the silence work for her, listening to her heart beat slowly and evenly as it had in the side corridor with Lauren.

“Very well,” Ani said finally, the words coming out bitterly. “We accept your terms, Lord Jack.”

“Good.” Jack smiled.

* * *

“Here is how this will go,” she said in Jack’s low rasp. “You all put the goods and the cards on the root over there, then take ten paces back. When you’re back, I’ll put down the stone. Nobody gets within arm’s reach of me.”

Tally drew a line on the moss ten paces from the root. The dark elves stopped half a pace short.

*Near enough,* Maureen told Tally silently.

*Nothing is near enough,* Tally responded, keeping the line lit.

Ani and her group brought the goods out bundle by bundle. The stone-blue flowers clicked together as they were set on the moss-covered root, like fine porcelain cups at a high tea. They were hard and cool to the touch, their petals a deep blue that reminded Maureen of slate slabs used in construction.

The flame ox hooves were wrapped in hide, but still gave off heat through the insulating leather. They smelled of a hot forge, the scent of iron and steel and molten rock wafting up as Maureen unwrapped them. For half a breath, she was back in Andrew’s yard again, soot blackening the man’s hands, the ring of iron on iron filling the air.

The centipede blood came in stoppered glass jars. The thick liquid swirled inside, a deep purple that caught the grey light like oil. The hearts of the black goats were packed in coarse salt, which crusted the wrapping and left a line of white grains on Maureen’s fingertips as she unwrapped them. The organs themselves were soft and wet against her fingers.

Lastly, Ani placed seven thin cards on the moss. They were made of low-grade stone, a pale grey colour. Each one had a stamped mark upon it.

Maureen crouched down on the wet ferns, picking up the waxed pouch first. Inside were three eggs, pale and leathery with faint blue veins running through their translucent shells. They were warm against her palm, not hot, but the warmth of something sleeping.

Tally checked the eggs as Maureen worked, first by sight, then by particle residue against the plate.

*Match by plate,* it said. *Not proof positive they’re viable.*

*I’ll have to find out the expensive way then,* Maureen responded silently.

*There are seven cards,* Tally said.

Maureen looked at each of them. The stamp on each card matched the stone card she had taken from Jay.

Tally compared the stamps. *The same as the card you took from Jay, within the margin of error for this grade of stone. I think we can trust these cards to be genuine.*

Maureen nodded, and reached inside her coat for the Bloodline Seal Stone. She pulled it out, feeling its blood red veins pulsing steadily against her gloved fingers. She tossed the stone to Ani in an underhand motion. Tally noted the beat leaving her hand, before reminding her of its ruling regarding the stone. *The Bloodline Seal Stone will only answer to the bloodline of the dark elves. Never to you, or any other outsider. While it was in your pocket it was worthless. You have just sold back to them their own key, at a most ruinous price.*

*I know, I know. But they think they got it cheap,* Maureen said silently.

Ani caught the stone with both hands, cradling it to her chest as though it were a small child. Her relief broke through everything else—her desperation, her pride, her grief. Maureen gathered up the goods, putting them into her pack beside the grenades. The dark elves watched every movement carefully. As she tucked the salt and animal hides away Maureen’s eyes rested on the waxed pouch for a moment. A small warmth moved behind Jack’s empty face, a private smile.

“There. The trade is done, so you can…” Maureen looked up. The dark elves were gone. The ferns were still swaying where they had been standing but now there were only dark shapes flickering between the trunks further off.

Maureen huffed a laugh.

*That was your voice,* Tally said.

*I know, but there wasn’t anyone left to hear me,* Maureen said.

*No, there wasn’t. But I could hear you,* Tally responded, its voice warm, just a hint of impish smugness in its tone.

Maureen let out one long slow breath, leaning back against the rough bark of the trunk. For the first time in hours, the muscles in her shoulders relaxed down from around her ears.

* * *

A little later, the clearing was emptied. The ferns had settled down and the flat grey sky was unchanged. The sweet heavy air hung unmoving. Ash’s smoke had thinned down to a single grey thread rising from the ground.

Maureen sat on the root, the waxed pouch resting on her knee. It was cool through its wrapping now. Her right hand was resting on top of it. The pouch was heavier than it appeared. Things always were when you’d wanted them for so long.

Tally displayed the ledger before her eyes.

[Cards: +7 (1,000 each). All columns: about 14,000.]

[Minai: 2 of 4 in hand.]

*This is the best day of my accounting life,* Tally said in its warm voice.

*You say that every time.*

*I know. And it keeps being true.*

Maureen sighed. She lifted her left hand off her knee and turned it palm down. A second Ancient Andas numeral had appeared on the back of that hand since this morning. It glowed faintly against her skin, small and neat as a stamp, next to her points glyph.

Tally read off the new numeral. [10]

*Ten,* Maureen repeated silently.

In its own voice Tally continued, *the numeral matches the end condition, and the fruits ripening in the valley this morning. I will be counting down from here. Ten days. I will maintain the following line on your display.* [Exit: 10 days]. *It will advance each dawn from now until the way out opens.*

Maureen considered that. Carter had said ninety out of every hundred must die before the way out would open. The fruits had ripened on the day the ninety was reached. Ten days after that, the way out would open. And every living hand in the realm would need to have the same number on it when that happened.

She imagined hundreds of hands across the ridges all counting down in synchronisation. A small cold satisfaction settled within her. After all this time, the rules were finally showing themselves.

She considered what she had seen while walking with the elite at the fog. The fiery red hair of Bevis had been among them. There had been apprentices above Frey’s column there. People who had watched Jack kill and were still alive.

Tally showed [Grenades: hand 8 / pack 26.]

*Thirty-four,* Tally said. *Against Bevis’s lot that is thin.*

*Everything is thin against Bevis’s lot.*

Tally read from its table again, *Twelve is within the top fifteen of any total I have seen on a living hand. Any more and you become interesting.*

*Then we stop.*

*That is the first time you have said so.*

She looked down at her hand where the number ‘12’ sat on the back. It was a name to fear, but a modest number. High points would mean Adrian looking at her with his kind eyes and asking questions. Everyone else would be asking with knives.

*No more hunting for me. I’ll spend the ten days sorting through my loot. Testing the eggs and letting my mana come back.*

Tally was silent for a moment, processing what she had decided. *Ten quiet days will more than refill your mana, many times over. I have never had ten quiet days to count before.*

*Enjoy the novelty while it lasts.*

She was going to choose to be ordinary. On purpose. It was like one of the routine bench tasks from Jing Sheng’s lab. Sit at the white table, label, sort, test and wait. Her body felt heavy. Her breathing slipped back into Silver Light without being asked. Three long breaths followed by one short, fingertips against her sternum through her cloak.

There was something almost restful about the decision to stop hunting.

* * *

Tally laid the map stitched from fourteen muster maps over her sight. She noted three ridges that had caves. All the ridges were well away from the valley. The farthest seemed the most promising. It was the only ridge where no one had recorded footprints crossing her lines. That would make it less likely people had been there recently.

*Fewer witnesses,* Tally noted. *My favourite kind of terrain.*

*Mine too,* she agreed silently.

Maureen Olsen slung the pack over her shoulders and felt the straps bite into her collarbones. The grenades sat hard against her spine and the ox hooves gave off a faint warmth through the hide. Freya's slender sword hung lightly at her hip, the balance point a finger's breadth forward of where her old blade had balanced. Then she ran, Jack's long strides eating up the distance between the trunks as the ferns closed behind her. At the last rise before she reached the ridge she paused to glance back once. Ash's smoke was just a thin thread of grey above the canopy. A moment later the trees obscured it from view.
