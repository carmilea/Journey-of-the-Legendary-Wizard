She stood there in the fallen pinewood, looking down at the ground where the fight had been. There were rings of scorched loam where Purgatory Fire had burned, and the slivers of blue ice that had been left by the Lament ticked softly as they melted into the moss. Of the doll itself, nothing remained but a smell like hot iron.

*She is gone from here,* Tally said. *I have no data on her elsewhere.*

*Then this is enough,* Maureen told her silently, turning her back on the clearing of ash.

She did not need to search for Joanna. A small red ember appeared on her overlay, painted by Tally: the thread of fire, lying dormant beneath the girl’s breastbone. Then Tally laid Joanna’s recorded signature over it.

[Seed: Joanna. 2.1 km NW.]

*You logged her,* Maureen said silently.

*Of course. I logged everyone you might one day have to kill.*

Maureen did not argue the point. Instead she began to walk north-west through the pines, towards the ember.

As she walked, Maureen pressed two fingers along her jawline, making sure that Richard’s weak chin was still seated properly, that the resonance of her voice in her throat had not drifted up out of its artificially lowered range. Her own short-cropped black hair prickled uncomfortably under her hood.

In a clearing of trampled bracken, the survivors were waiting. Their robes torn to rags, breaths coming short and shallow with fear, they huddled together, casting anxious glances back into the trees every few moments, as if waiting for the doll to step out after them.

Tally read them by density, a dozen particle fields flickering like water in a struck pan. One of them, a boy, flinched at the sound of a twig cracking under Maureen’s boot, and Tally noted that he would be the first to run.

“Joanna,” Maureen said. “It’s done. Come on out. We’re leaving.”

The woman started and looked over her shoulder. “Lord Rich— Richard.” She caught herself and quickly scrambled through the bracken to stand before Maureen. There was not a moment of hesitation before she left her friends behind. “Alice,” she whispered as soon as she was close enough. “Is she…?”

Maureen said nothing, just nodded once. That was all the confirmation Joanna needed. The tension went out of the woman’s body. The yoke lifted from her shoulders. She understood.

“What are you doing, sister?” came a voice from the edge of the trees. A girl ran forward, bits of twig caught in her long blonde hair. “Why did you come out? What if she’s still out there?” The girl cast a furious glare at ‘Richard’. “He’s just an ordinary apprentice, sister, how can you—”

“Christine!” The word snapped like a whip, and the younger girl bit back the rest of her sentence. But the look she gave Richard didn’t soften in the least. Maureen could see the beginnings of dislike flickering in Christine’s eyes. It would grow, take root, become established. This was going to be one more person who disliked Richard for no good reason.

*‘Ordinary’ is open to debate,* Tally noted.

*Let her keep it,* Maureen thought.

*You’ll see. Christine is going to be so embarrassed when she finds out. I’ve saved a slot for that.*

* * *

Only seven or eight apprentices remained with them on their journey west out of the forest. They were all friends of Joanna. The others had wandered away one by one, heading towards their own roads without a backward glance. No one had asked Richard to say his goodbyes.

Their road stretched out before them through the thinning forest. The ruts would be rimed with white frost when they rose in the mornings. The wet pine needles hissed and smoked as they burned at night. And every night Christine spread her blanket as far from Richard as possible, on the other side of the fire.

Maureen woke up on the second night to the sound of a chime in her head. Three dense sources were pacing around the camp, upwind. A heavier source lurked in the gully behind them.

*I have the ward, Maureen Olsen,* Tally said in her head. *I’ll keep it fed.*

Maureen rose silently and slipped out between the sleeping apprentices. She took care of the three apprentices a kilometre away from their campsite, with a low fireball and Freya’s slender sword. Then she buried what was left beneath leaf litter and was back under her blanket before the fire had gone out.

At dawn, Tally gave her a report. [Night: hostile apprentices 3, removed. High-energy creature 1, removed. Ward drew 0.2.]

Maureen brushed off her boot and said nothing as the apprentices woke up and prepared to break camp. And to Tally she silently said, *The same tomorrow, please.*

*Oh, that’s all right, Maureen Olsen!* Tally said in her mind, obviously quite pleased with herself. *You can count on me.*

And so it went for seven days. Over breakfast, the apprentices marveled at their good luck. They had encountered not one bit of trouble on their trip. One girl exclaimed that she’d never known such a clean road.

Joanna laughed. “We’ve just been lucky, I guess.”

*Lucky day indeed,* Maureen said silently to Tally.

*It is, Maureen Olsen,* Tally answered. *It is indeed. And you are the luck.*

On the seventh day, the trees ended abruptly. For Maureen, it felt as though the sky opened up above her, like someone lifting the lid off a pot. The wind suddenly smelled of wet soil and young wheat.

Emilia sat in the centre of a massive plain. Man-made channels flashed silver against the green fields of wheat, which waved like water in the wind. Tiny figures were visible, bent over in the rows. In the middle of this sea of vegetation stood a city, its pale stones shining in the sun. The heart of Emilia shimmered with ward-light.

Tally laid a grid of pale lines over the channels. [Airborne: 7 in a minute.] *I’ve never seen so many wizards wasting mana on a commute,* she said.

The road was busy with carriages pulled by horned beasts, carts full of pedlars squealing on dry axles, and knights in dusty mail armour. A boy dressed in robes flew low over a wagon filled with hay, and the carter didn’t even bother to look up.

Joanna leaned close to ‘Richard,’ her voice quiet. “It’s a wizards’ city, but it can’t function without regular people. It’s mixed, really.” She paused for a moment. “But only the wizards are really . . . in control.”

*Mixed city under absolute power,* Tally filed away. *I’ve seen that ledger before. Skran.*

*Like the sewage lanes running downhill from the Duke’s chipped gilt,* Maureen thought. She nodded once.

When they reached the city gate, the pale stone had warmed in the sun. The air smelled like bread ovens and horse dung. The ward-light tickled along Maureen’s forearms, barely more than the beginning of a rash.

Inside the walls, Joanna’s friends said their goodbyes. Two of them thanked Richard, though awkwardly, not quite sure what they were thanking him for. Joanna led her sister and Maureen deeper into the city, down streets that grew narrower and more crowded as Tally counted the turns.

* * *

Joanna stopped before a gate carved from stone. She took a deep breath. “This is where the Jadeleaf Ring lives,” she said, making it sound like an introduction to a relative everyone pitied.

Maureen narrowed her eyes, focusing on the gateway. It was grand in its bones, a sweep of columns carved with leaves, but every groove was filled with dust. The first step up to the gate was marble, but it had cracked clean across at some point. The green banners hanging from the gate’s sides had faded to the color of very old tea.

*Three lamps unlit, no sign of recent cleaning, dust undisturbed. Crack is years old, moss growing in it. This place hasn’t been looked after for a long time, Maureen. Not lately. Long ago.*

Joanna’s face grew soft and sad. Christine pressed her lips together and, for once, didn’t have anything to say. There wasn’t any other sound in the courtyard, not even birds. Somewhere inside, a shutter hung loose, knocking against the building with the breeze.

Then voices raised in anger sounded from behind the wooden doors. “What’s happening?” Joanna asked.

“Come on!”

The sisters ran for the door. Maureen hesitated, glancing up at the faded green banner over the door, before following at a more sedate pace.

Inside, a knot of apprentices surrounded an old man. “Three hundred and more, Senge. Don’t you think it’s time?”

“Yeah, Steward.” Another chimed in. “The Ring’s still in the Wizard Academy Alliance, ain’t it? Sanctions, old man.”

*Official Wizard,* Tally said, reading the densities in the room. *Old. His density is thinner than your Barok sample, but not elementalised. Danger: weak. The leader of the pack is Second-Class, middle column.*

The old man’s face turned grey-green. “We borrowed a hundred from you, Ace. One hundred. How is that three hundred?”

The greasy young man grinned and tapped the paper lying on the table, weighed down by a faintly glowing magic stone. “Read the interest, Steward. It’s all there. You signed it.” He was enjoying himself too much to try hiding it.

The old wizard’s color came and went, but no words came out.

Maureen watched an Official Wizard choke on a scrap of paper presented by an apprentice. It was more interesting than sad.

“Enough, Ace!” Joanna stepped forward, her face pale.

Ace’s eyes lit up like candles. They ran slowly over Joanna, then Christine, and they stayed on Christine.

“Don’t want to pay? Fine. You and Christine come and keep me company for a few days. Both of you. I’ll tear this up and give you a hundred on top.”

Both sisters paled. Old Senge roared “Ace!” and the word cracked in his throat.

The young man just laughed at him. “Oh, don’t be like that, Steward. The Ring’s finished. Think it over, girls.”

Something cold moved in Maureen’s chest. She was back on the ship again, listening to the rumours about the girls who were traded for food. She saw Lucy and Alice’s untouched rations. Her body moved forward before she made any decisions.

Ace finally noticed her. “And who are you?” His face darkened. “Get out.” He reached for her shoulder, ready to pin her down like a stray dog.

A line of Tally’s warning was only half drawn across Maureen’s display when the field made its decision for her. Dense dark particles bloomed off of Maureen’s skin, shining with an oily glow that Maureen had never seen before, and Ace’s hand stopped a finger’s breadth away. The recoil threw spasms up his arm and he screamed.

*Oh,* Tally said, sounding just a little put out. *You didn’t need me for that.*

*I’ll need you for the rest,* Maureen told her silently.

This was the first time the field had ever moved without being asked. A low hum ran along her bones. It was her own power, reacting to an insult all by itself. She liked it. Perhaps more than her face showed.

Maureen lifted her boot and drove it into the greasy young man’s stomach. He folded in half and flew back across the hall, skidding along the flagstones until he hit a bench and stopped.

His companions were shouting. Two of them rushed over and hauled him up by the arms. The rest turned on Maureen, hands rising and mouths already shaping chants.

*Left two. Then the one with the knife.* A small amber mark appeared over each target in turn.

Maureen didn’t even have to say the words any more. She just had to look.

A Blast Fireball of flame exploded in the air between the apprentices, a hissing Undead Wind Arrow ripped from nowhere, and a Corrupting Black Cloud burst over their heads. For an Official Wizard, Rank Zero work was nothing. They swallowed the group whole. There was the dull thud of the fireball, the sigh of the arrow, the stink of rotting vegetation from the cloud. Flagstones cracked beneath scorch marks, a bench split in two, and apprentices sprawled across the floor, groaning.

*Six down, six breathing. Leg meat only, so minimal blood loss. No data on Emilia law regarding wizards engaging in conflict,* Tally supplied.

*Then nobody dies today,* Maureen answered silently.

“Richard, how did you—”

“You—”

Joanna and Christine stood frozen as the apprentices began to moan and pick themselves up off the floor. Maureen turned to face them. She looked calm, implacable.

“Something you wish to say?” she asked. “That lot were about to attack me. I dealt with them first.”

Her eyes moved past Joanna to the old man.

“I am curious, though,” she said. “An Official Wizard, allowing apprentices to spit at him. Interesting.”

The old man sighed. “Young wizard, you do not understand our position. I am old, and there is much to consider.”

*This is what it looks like from the outside when you don’t climb,* Tally said quietly.

Maureen’s pulse ticked once, and she thought about junk methods and capped apprentices. And how many wizards never even made it that far. They just stopped.

There was no point hiding any more. Maureen reached up and lifted the lid she had been holding pressed down for weeks. Her aura exploded into the hall. Every apprentice in the Jadeleaf Ring felt it. The plain young man was an Official Wizard.

Christine’s face went the colour of whey.

*Opening the slot now.*

Maureen exchanged a few civil words with Senge, then allowed Joanna to show her to a guest room. She stepped over Ace’s legs on the way out.

* * *

Someone had worked hard on the chairs. They shone with new polish, their cushions were darned with neat little stitches, but the damp still crept through the beeswax smell.

Senge stood up with a chipped glass in his hand, and despite the years that dragged his shoulders down and the grey white hair he was a handsome man, with eyes that sparkled. “Wizard Richard. It is an honour to have you with us at the Jadeleaf Ring.”

“The honour is mine,” Maureen replied, raising her own glass a fraction.

The welcome feast was veal, white bread and mushroom soup, and nothing more. When Maureen tore off a piece of the white bread, the image of Carey snatching Aaron’s loaf on the ship floated up for half a breath before sinking back down. *I will log it as bread,* Tally said. *Thank you,* Maureen replied silently, and ate.

Tally counted the apprentices down the table. *Ten, if you are generous. I am being generous.*

Maureen put down her spoon and made up her mind about the place. It was a decaying organisation. A shell that still knew enough to set a table when guests arrived.

The apprentices watched her with a mixture of fear and hope. One thin boy kept refilling Maureen’s glass even before it was half empty, his hand shaking just enough to rattle the bottle neck against the rim.

Christine sat and ate in silence and never once looked up. Joanna barely ate at all and her eyes kept sliding across to Senge.

*Even his glass has a chip,* Tally said. *The worst chip of any. And his hand only shakes when he holds the glass up to make the toast.*

Laughter around the table was thin, and evaporated quickly. Candles burnt down faster than conversations.

Maureen thanked Senge for his hospitality, excused herself early and made her way back upstairs to her room.

* * *

Maureen sat down on the creaking bed, taking off her boots. A damp stain spread out from the corner like a map of a land nobody wanted to visit and her ward hummed softly along the doorframe.

Tally was able to give warning before anyone knocked on the door. The corridor outside was dark and it took a moment for Tally to pick out the single source of density approaching, but the signature was Joanna’s. Joanna’s pulse was elevated but the ember of the seed under her breastbone was steady.

Maureen frowned as she rose and opened the door before Joanna had a chance to raise her hand to knock.

“Joanna.”

“Wizard Richard. My lord.” Joanna bowed her head. Her voice sounded tight, forced into a channel too small for it. “I just wanted to ask you to help the Ring through this.”

“We’ve no money, and the Steward is old. He is an Official Wizard, but no one fears him any more. You are young, you are strong.” She bent almost double with the force of her bow. “The Jadeleaf Ring requests your protection.”

Maureen looked down at the bowed neck. In the carriage Joanna had sunk to her knees to offer her a bondservice oath. Maureen had turned it down. Now though, she saw it for what it was. The same move, made a little earlier in the play.

Tally laid it side by side for her. The bondservice oath offered and declined. Christine’s apology never made. The ledger was balanced, Tally said. *No debt owed on either side.*

Maureen felt nothing. Not for the girl who cowered before her. Not for the Ring or its fate. Just the cost. A bond, a debt, an Alliance with sanctions and a bruised creditor who would most likely have friends. Every thread she took up was another that someone else could pull.

“Protect the Jadeleaf Ring?” Maureen smiled faintly. “Sorry. Not interested.”

Joanna’s face changed. “My lord Richard—”

Maureen talked over her. “Joanna. It’s late. Go and get some rest.” She turned and walked back into her room, letting the door swing shut behind her. It obeyed, slamming closed with enough force to echo down the dark corridor outside.

*That was your field,* Tally told her.

*I meant it to,* Maureen responded silently.

*Maureen’s field, effect: shutting door, intent: shutting door. Logged as meant.*

Outside there was a long silence before footsteps sounded, walking slowly away down the corridor. Maureen pinched out the candle and lay back in the dark, listening to the ward humming on the door.
