Maureen emerged from the trees into the saddle. She heard laughter ahead of her, light and careless, and stopped as she reached the edge of the bracken. The sound was unfamiliar. There had been screams and shouts, laughter forced and wild from too much drink or too many drugs, but not this easy, free laughter for days.

She stood quietly, her eyes scanning the open ground before her. It looked like a garden party. A band of young men and women lounged about, chatting, laughing, and drinking. One young man sat on a cloak folded on the ground, two more leaned against each other, shoulder to shoulder, sharing a flask that passed between them. None stood as sentry, none had a hand near a weapon. They were in the middle of a slaughterhouse.

They wore robes of good cloth, lined with silk. The flat grey light reflected off silver pins fastened to collars and breasts. One young man even peeled a pear with a little knife. The sweet, wet smell of the fruit cut through the faint taste of boiling sugar in the air. Their boots were clean, she noticed. Not a speck of mud on any of them.

[Hostiles: 18.]

Tally had finished its count before Maureen had finished looking.

[Third-Class: 11. Twelfth borderline by calibration.]

[Knights: mixed.]

Tally highlighted the thin young man at the front of the group. The others glanced at him before they laughed, as if checking to make sure it was all right.

The silver pins were wizard-family crests, she realised. Beside each of the young men and women with the crests stood larger, plainer apprentices. These had no crest at all, and always stood a half step behind someone who did.

*Friends of the houses, she thought. Hired muscle with good manners.*

*So the families do it too.* There was a dry little twist inside her as she watched the group laugh and chat, oblivious to her presence. She'd been expecting the orders' elites to band up and sweep away the weak. Of course blood would band with blood. There were Blue Ring houses here, and Dark Forest houses. Outside the realm they were enemies, but inside they were cousins.

*How long do you think their truce will last?* she asked Tally silently.

[No data.]

*Still, I would bet on the hour that the last stranger dies.*

*As would I. They haven't even thought as far as tomorrow.*

Tally cross-referenced one sigil - a coiled mark on a youth's collar - against the seal on Jay's confidentiality scroll.

[Olivia Family. Match: Jay's scroll.]

*These are cousins of your friends.*

*They are not my friends.*

*Would 'enemies' cousins be more accurate?*

*Accurate, yes.*

Maureen's eyes narrowed and her jaw set. Slowly, her left hand clenched at her side. Beneath her breastbone sat something cold and level. It was like the last count Tally had given her, standing at the pit, listening to Jay's screams ticking down to nothing.

The freckled apprentice spotted her then. He saw her standing alone among the trees, a lone man in a scorched grey cloak. His face lit up like a cat spotting a mouse-hole.

"Oh, look," he said, his voice full of delight. "Somebody's lost."

Heads turned lazily. Eyes regarded her without interest, reading Jack's aura through the cloaking mask. "Third-Class," said a girl, tilting her head. "Huh. Not bad."

"Third-Class is still one," the pear-eater said without looking up from his knife.

The heavy youth was already rolling his shoulders, loosening up his arms. "Mine. Leonina hasn't had any since breakfast."

"Fatso had two," said the girl with the thin, quick voice, "and he popped the second one's head before I'd even had any fun. This one's Walker's."

"Hanos saw him first," drawled the freckled apprentice without bothering to raise his voice.

The rest of the band laughed as though this settled it. They carved her up the way a family might carve a roast at the table, dividing it according to precedence.

She stood and listened to herself being portioned out, feeling very little about it. What she noticed instead were small things: the way the heavy youth stood with all his weight on the ball of his front foot, the way the girl kept her hands tucked up her sleeves.

*Walker. That's Lilith's family,* Tally said quietly.

*I know,* Maureen said back silently.

*You just touched the pouch where you keep Lilith's notebook.*

*It's not there.*

*I know.*

The pear-eater flicked his core into the grass by her boots without even glancing to see where it fell. She counted loose stances, turned backs, and drinkers with both hands full. This was prey that thought it was hunting.

"So," said someone from the back of the group, "how shall we play with the poor bastard?"

"Slowly," said another, and the laughter rumbled round again, careless and loud in the silent air.

Tally laid the geometry out flat before Maureen. No one had drawn. The eighteen hunters stood in a loose crescent, the nearest of them twelve paces away. A broad spread of shells would take out most of the front rank.

*Keep that count ready,* she told Tally silently.

Beneath the peals of laughter from the hunters, the trial realm remained silent as death. Not a bird sang, not an insect buzzed. Only Maureen's pulse ticked away, slow and unhurried.

Tally showed her this too, a steady beat, imperturbable as a clock.

* * *

The thin, freckled apprentice strolled forward, tapping his chest just above his breastbone. On his grey robe there was pinned a silver pin, shaped like some heraldic badge or other. He seemed to think it would make her bow, or kneel, or do something that would acknowledge his importance. "Watson," he said. "Hanos."

He let that hang for a moment. When it became clear that she wasn't going to react, he looked faintly disappointed. "Doesn't matter whose dog you are, friend. Blue Ring, the Forest, some swamp order nobody's heard of." He smiled. "Nobody's coming. Nobody even knows you're standing here. All that's left to decide is how long it takes."

"Long," someone called out, and the hunters laughed.

"Oh?" Maureen asked, speaking in Jack's low rasp. "Is that so?"

Something cold surfaced in her eyes. Her hand made one small movement, and suddenly three alchemical grenades were resting in her fingers. The shells were warm from lying against her skin beneath her cloak. They were heavy in her palm, their black surfaces rough against her thumb. They smelled faintly of road iron. The trigger word rested on her tongue, waiting for release.

Her plan was about as complex as the grenades themselves: blow them apart, and sort out the rest later.

"Break his legs first," said Watson, almost kindly. "Then we'll take our time."

He began to chant. A pendant hanging around his neck responded with a soft blue glow. It was an enchanted item—something crafted by wizards, which the boy's family had thoughtfully hung around his neck.

Tally heard the chant, caught the pendant's glow through Maureen's sight, and flashed the analysis forward.

> Item-boosted. Lightning. Wide. Grenade window: 1.2 s.

Watson spoke the final line of his spell: "Yarlei-Monsso." Then he closed his hands in a sign, and the air crackled. A great sheet of blue lightning exploded out from the apprentice's hand, spreading wide as it approached Maureen. The spell formed a net, crackling with electricity. It rolled across the bracken toward her, filling the air with the smell of a struck storm.

Hair rose on Maureen's arms beneath her cloak. The net popped and spat like fat on a hot griddle. Blue light washed across the grass, illuminating everything in stark, harsh detail.

"Fool," she breathed.

Her wrist cocked back, ready to throw. The word Kerrash already hovered on her tongue.

Then, suddenly, something blocked her view. A shadow fell between her and the net. The lightning reached it with a soft whoomp sound, and then was gone. The spell evaporated in a single breath, as if it had never been cast.

The world went silent.

Silence rang in Maureen's ears. Afterimages of blue lightning danced across her vision, leaving trails like snail slime. A line of smoking bracken lay at her feet. On the other side of the open saddle, the hunters' laughter choked off in midbreath.

[Warning. HIGH energy radiation source. New.]

The first high-band alert that Tally had ever given her within the realm, and it was coming from above. Maureen looked up, and saw figures descending from the grey cloud, their black robes flapping gently as they drifted down. The hems of their robes stirred slightly, despite the still air. They fell slowly, in no hurry at all. In their center, a man in a silver robe descended as well. His long hair floated around his face, drifting in some unseen wind. When his feet touched the grass, he raised his head to look at her, and she recognized him.

It was the same face, the same fine aristocratic lines. But it looked borrowed.

Heat radiated from him. It rolled out from his body in a slow wave, washing over her. She couldn't see it, but she could feel it, a prickling heat against her skin, burning out from the inside.

He took a single step toward her, and her entire body twitched in response, trying to get away before her mind even made a conscious decision to do so.

*That is him, not you,* Tally said, its tone cold and terse. *Your body thinks that it is standing next to an open forge.*

*Am I in danger?* she asked silently.

*I have no data on this version of him. Your knees appear to be making the correct decision.*

The man smiled at Maureen. It was the smile of an old friend, one who had seen her just yesterday morning as they parted ways outside of a lecture hall. "Long time no see, Maureen."

[Witnesses to name: 18 + escort 9.]

Maureen's body became rigid as a statue. Her borrowed face, dyed hair, and mask covering her aura were still all in place. And he had looked right through them all. Now her real name hung in the open air of a killing ground.

She waited another moment, still as death, then nodded once. "Aaron."

Behind him, the escort in black robes stood with heads bowed, like servants waiting deferentially on their lord. That display of deference shook Maureen more than the lightning ever could have.

*How?* she asked Tally silently. *How did he see through my disguise?*

*Face, hair, and mask are all holding per readout specifications. I have no data on how he was able to bypass them.*

Tally didn't sound happy about that, and Maureen wasn't happy to hear it.

"Now, now," Aaron said lightly. "It's all right, Maureen. Leave this to me." He turned his back on her, regarding the hunters with a calm expression.

Watson managed to recover his sneer first. "Oh, there's two of them. You're this Maureen's little friend, then?" He cast his gaze over the surrounding trees. "Look around, idiot. Count us, if you can count. And you still float down here like you—"

Watson exploded where he stood.

One moment his mouth was forming the next word, the next moment there was a wet clap of bursting flesh and nothing left above the collar. His body remained standing for a heartbeat longer, then collapsed into the bracken.

A fine red mist settled over the grass, spattering the pear-eater's silk robe with freckles of blood. The little knife dropped from his fingers, forgotten, and Maureen felt her fingers tighten around the shells. Her palm hurt as the iron bit into her flesh. Watson had been a strong Third-Class, but he was gone with a lazy flick of the wrist.

Aaron let his hand drop back to his side.

"So tiresome," he said softly, almost to himself. Then, looking around the circle of faces, he added with what sounded like genuine amusement, "Blue Ring and the Dark Forest. Both in one meadow. Isn't that lucky."

One young man stumbled forward, his face flushed with anger that had yet to be tempered by fear. "You—do you know who—he's O—"

He did not finish the sentence. Aaron never even glanced in his direction, but the young man's chest bulged beneath his robes, tearing the cloth, then burst into a fountain of red that spattered the grass before the boy collapsed. His legs twitched for a moment before lying still.

"That's Pat, that's—that was Pat, you can't just—" Another one began, backing away, slipping in the blood that was soaking into the bracken. Aaron took a single step toward him, not hurrying, and brought his open palm down on the young man's head as if swatting a fly. There was a sick thud that echoed up through the soles of Maureen's boots. Where the young man had stood there was now a flattened ruin of red in the high grass. Her stomach turned over once, then settled. She realized that she was breathing through her mouth, not wanting to taste the wet copper smell of blood.

The third young man's teeth were chattering so hard that Maureen could hear them clicking across the distance that separated them. He managed to force out one word. "Y-you—" Then there was a muffled pop from within the robes, and the boy folded like a sack dropped from a height.

*Warm again,* Tally said. *Witnesses: 14.*

*Shut up,* she told it silently.

*Very well, but I will maintain a quiet count should you require it later.*

The escort stood behind Aaron, nine figures wrapped in black robes. They made a glacier of darkness behind him, cold and unmoving. No one met Aaron's eyes. The faces of the remaining apprentices had taken on the same grey tone as the sky overhead, and someone was being quietly ill on the other side of the meadow.

One of the hunters broke. "We're wizard-family heirs! Apprentices of the Blue Ring and the Dark Forest! You have no right! By what right!" His voice cracked into a sob and he collapsed into the tall grass, sitting down hard, looking around at the bodies, half laughing and half weeping. Waiting for death.

Aaron didn't strike him. Instead, he bowed his head slightly so that his face was hidden by the silver robe. For a long moment, he stood there, then he gave a small laugh, though there was something sour beneath the amusement.

"Why would I kill you?" Aaron said, finally. "Let me tell you a story."

* * *

"My story," Aaron said, conversationally, like two old friends sitting over a bottle of wine in the evening. "Let's say there was a boy. A count's son. Little principality, nobody you'd have heard of. Grew up next door to a duke's daughter and decided, when he was six years old, that he'd marry her one day. Very serious boy. Wrote it down, I think.

"He would've done it too. Good family, good sword arm, all the right manners. He was going to keep her safe all her life." He smiled down at the high grass, his face twisted and bitter. "Stories like that are supposed to end with the wedding. This one didn't."

The survivors weren't even breathing, as if afraid to disturb the quiet air. Maureen stood with her shells clutched in her fist, not moving at all, and the stillness supported her, like armour keeping a body standing after death.

"They were both talented, as it turned out. Some Blue Ring apprentices passing through scooped them up, and on the road through Skran lands they collected a few more strays." His eyes flicked toward her for a moment before moving away. "Nice people, some of them. It didn't matter. Not at first."

She knew every face that he didn't name. The stream, Aaron and Richard playing in the water, splashing each other. The carriages rolling along the road, creaking beneath their weight, leaving deep ruts in the dust. The girl who refused to leave her carriage, keeping herself separate from the rest of the wizard-seed batch, and Aaron's thumb, working ceaselessly on the hem of his sleeve, wearing the threads away until they were thin and frail. She watched his thumb now. It didn't move. It was perfectly still.

"Then there was the test." He smiled again. "She came out middle grade. That was good. There was a piece of trash who'd been sniffing around her since they left the road and came out high. And the boy . . ." He tipped his head back and let out a long breath before he said the word, as if the taste had gone bad in his mouth. "Low."

Tally didn't add anything to this, but a small line appeared at the edge of Maureen's display, set down on the bench like a sample waiting to be analysed:

[Carey: grade 7. Aaron: 3. Ellena: 4.]

"So she drifted away. They fought, really fought. Not one of those fake fights where you slap each other and make up half an hour later. No, they said things that you can't take back, things you wish you could cut out of your brain. But the trash was so patient." His smile thinned even further, becoming a bitter line across his face. "Then one day she walked into his carriage and gave him her most precious thing.

"The boy went mad."

Maureen remembered the night that she and Richard had held Aaron down, kept him from going after Carey. She remembered the bucking of his body beneath them, the sudden dead weight when he'd given up, and the terrible sounds he'd made, weeping into the dirt.

She knew what he was talking about. She shook her head slightly, letting out one long, slow breath from her nose. That was the only emotion she allowed herself to show.

At 'her most precious thing' Tally said nothing at all. Then, very quietly, it spoke. *He's leaving out the knife.*

*So am I,* Maureen thought silently.

Beneath the surface, though, she saw the ship's deck. White bread snatched from a hand. Aaron smiling as he ran Carey through from behind. Her own decision to stand right where she was.

Tally did not need to say logged.

"Then a proud, powerful Dark Forest wizard stole the entire batch of seeds. For a game, I'd imagine. Long journeys are so dull."

He laughed softly.

"And the boy got a mentor.

"Other mentors taught. His taught with his fists. Curses, little humiliations in front of the others. A fresh bruise before the last one healed. And with the more gifted seeds, that same man was gentle as a grandfather. Well, why waste kindness on low-aptitude trash? It's always aptitude. Always."

Maureen remembered purple bruises peeking out from beneath collar and cuff, in the stone alcove so long ago. And she remembered the line Tally had left open since then.

[Causes of bruising: unknown.]

Tally silently altered the line.

[Causes of bruising: mentor.]

"So the boy became trash. Lost the girl. Lost himself." Tears gleamed in his eyes. "Nothing left." He began laughing, high and cracked, his shoulders shaking. "Nothing left, and then do you know what happened?" His tears were flowing freely now, but still he laughed. "Heaven felt sorry for him! Heaven! It gave him blood. Purgatory's blood. You pray your entire lives for one more grade, and he got something better than any grade any of you will ever have.

"Every one of you. Every one of them. Apprentice, Wizard, it makes no difference to me. Every single person who laughed at him, every single person who hit him—" His voice suddenly stopped, choked off. When it returned, it was little more than a whisper. "I'm going to make them pay for everything. I'm king now. Do you hear me? He's come back."

Maureen's particle sense was pressed up against him, like a hand held against a closed furnace door.

*Above Frey,* Tally told her.

*No Wizard samples to compare against,* it said. *Adrian's are the nearest match.*

*Don't put them in the same column.*

*Separate columns. Noted.*

Aaron rose from the grass, his feet leaving the ground, and thick, rolling streams of sulphur smoke poured down from where his heels should have been. The smoke didn't dissipate as it fell—the streams grew thicker and more distinct. They twisted and curled beneath him, drawing together until there was the vast phantom of a giant black serpent lying coiled beneath Aaron's body.

The heat that had prickled Maureen's skin was now surging toward her, a wall of it, and she knew this heat. It was the same dry sensation that had stung her cheeks when she'd first fired a Blast Fireball in Room 21. The first time her own power had burned her. Small flames ran along the coils of the phantom serpent, like sparks running along a fuse.

The jaws of the serpent parted, and it hissed. The sound didn't quite reach her ears. Instead it came from inside her, a buzzing in her breastbone that reminded her of Evans's hum on the ship. Every survivor on the saddle flinched at once, as though they'd all felt it.

The phantom serpent opened its eyes. Cold, earth-yellow eyes with vertical slit pupils. Aaron's own eyes became the same color, and his silver robe began to whip about his body as if a gale lived within the cloth.

>Match: Fasol serpent / Aaron's road phantom / the burned Blue Ring corpse.

>Black Mando line, per Gregory's sketch.

>Confidence: high.

>Close is now certain.

*Keep his file open,* Maureen thought silently.

*It has not been closed.*

"Once, I was that boy," Aaron said. His voice was soft, but it reached every ear upon the saddle. "But that doesn't matter anymore. My name is Aaron. I've come back."

The survivors knelt in the red bracken, faces grey and drawn. The escort stood behind Aaron, silent figures in black. The three shells in Maureen's fist were hot from her own grip, but she didn't put them away.

Above the slow, languid coils of the black serpent, the grey cloud did not move, and Tally's quiet count sat at the edge of Maureen's display, where she couldn't stop seeing it.
