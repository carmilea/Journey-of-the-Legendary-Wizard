Maureen’s progress through the southeast quarter of Red Rock Town was fast and silent and she found nothing. There were no signs of attacks on the houses, their shutters barred and fast against the night, doorposts bearing fresh claw marks, once a dead dog lying in a gutter. The stink of sulphur from the corpses of the day was still evident, though becoming fainter as she made her way lane by lane. Nineteen lanes of nineteen checked, and no sign, Tally informed her. Nothing on file either. Burton said they were coming from the southeast, but didn’t say from within Red Rock Town’s walls.

Don’t be smug, she told it silently.

Smug? I am just being precise.

Maureen frowned, and glanced past the last of the rooftops to the dark fields beyond. Then she swung herself over the low wall and set off running. Southeast. A faint shadow in the night, making no more sound than a waft of breeze, her breath condensing in the cold air, her lungs expanding with each stride, the dry stubble of the field crunching underfoot. The few lights of Red Rock Town behind her, dwindling with every step.

* * *

Some way out her hand grew cold. The sigil surfaced in her palm, glowing faint silver.

Maureen Olsen, the silver sigil has come up unbidden again, as it did over the sulphur corpses. I still have no data on why.

Then we follow it, she answered silently, picking up her pace, the sigil growing brighter until it shone like moonbeams in her hand. When it was brightest, she halted at the edge of a paupers’ graveyard.

It was a sad place. There were some old slate markers, furred with lichen, next to newer wooden ones. The ground smelt damp and musty. Somewhere in the distance a dog barked. Wilted grave flowers lay scattered here and there. Maureen ignored the graves themselves, and focused on one plain boulder among several that sat at the front of the burial ground. Her eyes narrowed. A concealment spell lay over its surface.

Its structure and energy reactions are completely different to anything on file. The node layout is also unique. I warn you now, this is beyond me.

I know, she replied silently. This is very strange. Could be a long night’s work.

She held her glowing palm a finger’s width away from the rough stone. She paused for a moment, her breath held, feeling the cold breath of the boulder on her skin. With one swift movement, she brushed it with the sigil.

The stone shivered beneath her touch.

Layers of runes rose all around her, like reflections dancing on the surface of still water.

The boulder was gone.

“Undone. Just like that?” she murmured, staring at the pitch black tunnel mouth that yawned before her.

I am unable to explain why a key will fit a lock I have never seen before, Maureen Olsen.

Makes two of us then, she answered silently as she felt a current of cold air flow out from the tunnel. It smelt of stone dust, and something ancient. Her pulse was running quick now, and she smiled.

* * *

She reached into her coat pocket and drew out the grey-white stone tablet that was the size of her palm. The tablet, dormant, was one of the oddments that had filled Patton’s cave niche.

Filth-Star Slate. A living stone tablet, it is a famous troublemaker, loved and hated by wizards in equal measure. Possesses superb scouting skills and has an unparalleled ability to sense traps woven into spells. A live specimen is a valuable material for certain experiments...

...that is why I kept it, she told Tally silently.

Maureen began chanting softly, and a stream of dense dark energy particles poured into the stone tablet. They ran cold across her fingers, but the stone beneath them grew warm. A smell, like flint struck by steel, rose from the slate.

The stone grew to its full size. Four thin legs, clad in red-and-white shoes, sprouted from the base of the tablet. Two small hands appeared, and a pair of large eyes blinked open. The Filth-Star Slate babbled nonsense for a moment before it caught sight of Maureen. It jumped in fright, then turned and ran.

Tally placed the correct wording of the Ancient Andas oath in front of Maureen’s eyes, and coached her tongue back onto the binding syllable.

Got it, Maureen told Tally silently.

“By my power, I grant you life. By the oath, you shall heed my summons.”

The Filth-Star Slate froze where it stood. Its little red-and-white shoes scuffed against the wet clay as it tried to keep moving despite Maureen’s command. It rolled its large eyes at her, looking sulky.

Maureen laid Pupil-Sight on the living stone tablet. The Filth-Star Slate trudged into the tunnel on its four little legs. It didn’t want to move, but it had no choice. Maureen Olsen sat down on the low stone kerb by the side of the tunnel mouth and closed her eyes. The Filth-Star Slate’s little red-and-white shoes disappeared into the dark.

Behind her eyelids, the world viewed by the Filth-Star Slate unfolded before her. It was a grainy picture that tilted whenever the slate moved.

* * *

There was a dizzying lurch as the slate suddenly stood before a manor house. The grey stone walls glimmered faintly with defensive spells.

Tally’s relayed vision wavered slightly. There was little power behind the magic that ran the slate’s body, and the colours that it saw were thin and insubstantial. Dead ivy clung to the ancient stones like dried blood, the roof tiles were a mottled grey from the lichen that grew upon them. The manor was old. Tally estimated its age from the weathering of the stone, the drifts of dust in the slate’s feed, and the decay that it could see in the structure, compared to the ruins it had on record. At least three or four centuries old. Possibly five. The error band on Tally’s estimate was large, but Maureen filed the information away silently.

Maureen took control of the Filth-Star Slate’s movements herself as it walked up the pathway to the house. There were many spell traps along the way, ancient but still effective, and the Filth-Star Slate dodged around them with ease.

[Live traps passed: 7]

Tally helpfully noted how many traps it had spotted from their glinting aura, and how many times the slate had sidestepped to avoid them. It felt each trap before I did, admitted Tally.

Troublemakers have their uses, Maureen told Tally silently.

The tiny red-and-white shoes scampered nimbly up the path, kicking up dust from the cracked flagstones. There were black scorch marks on the ground where ancient traps had fired when some unlucky soul had triggered them.

Pages littered the courtyard in front of the manor, thrown out by a frustrated or irritated owner. The Filth-Star Slate held one of the pages up for Tally and Maureen to examine.

Yellowed paper, cramped ink, lines drawn through with such force they had torn the page.

Maureen scanned the sheet quickly. It was covered in hypotheses and frameworks for spells, all relatively simple.

These go past anything in Official Wizard texts that I have on record, Tally said in its own voice.

Perhaps this was an Official Wizard’s home, Maureen thought silently to herself.

There was a sudden gnawing sound. The ground rippled, and more small mouths filled with sharp teeth erupted from the soil. Devil Vine. Grafted into the ground here, fixed in perpetuity, it would be far stronger than ordinary Devil Vine.

Dust pressing flat. Gravity.

The line flashed on Tally’s display just a fraction of a second before the Filth-Star Slate should have been crushed.

Jump! Maureen thought silently, and the Filth-Star Slate strained to leap into the air.

But it could not move.

The gravity spell pinned it down.

A glowing energy arrow streaked through the air and struck the Filth-Star Slate. It exploded into a hail of fragments, which the sharp-toothed mouths eagerly devoured.

Crunching sounds of stone filled her ears, and the feed began to flicker and fray as the connection grew tenuous. Her jaw clacked down hard on the cold grave kerb, and she swore aloud at the failure. It was annoying to lose such a good asset, but she wasn’t afraid of the consequences. Not yet, anyway.

As her sight began to fade, a glowing translucent figure appeared at the doorway, and he laughed. “Though I am only a Third-Class Apprentice, my residence is not someplace that can be broken into by anyone. Not even Official Wizards dare to walk here carelessly.” He stopped speaking abruptly, his face suddenly looking lost.

Tally remained silent, a steady presence behind her eyes. Maureen held still, listening carefully.

“So you are no random intruder,” the figure said. “You are an inheritor.”

He paused for a moment before continuing in a murmur. “Several hundred years, and finally someone has obtained the Teacher’s book. Decoded it. Condensed the inheritance mark.” The translucent figure shook his head, sighing, looking half glad and half sad at the same time.

A cold thrill ran up Maureen’s arms, the sigil in her palm prickling at the mention of a Third-Class Apprentice who had written spells beyond even Official Wizard texts.

The vision went dark. Pupil-Sight was gone.

* * *

Maureen felt the backlash from the broken spell slam into her. She grunted with the force, staggering back a step or two, catching herself by putting a hand on the cold and rough rock. She gasped for breath, feeling the night wind cold against her sweat dampened skin.

Her chest clenched with pain, and she felt the blood surge and churn in her ears, leaving a taste of iron in her mouth.

Tally monitored her body by what little its own body could tell it, feeling the pounding pulse in her head, the tightness in her chest, the shaking of her hands, and identified the phenomenon as backlash. It had no data to indicate whether the effects would be long lasting or not.

I’ll live, Maureen thought back silently.

I will hold you to that, Tally responded.

Tally began to count Maureen’s breaths in her head, helping her to regulate them, to slow them down to the rhythm of the Silver Light. In…two…three…four, hold…one. Out…two…three…four. Maureen followed Tally’s count until her hands stopped trembling. The black hole in the rock remained before her, and the sigil had faded to a mere faint cold in her palm.
