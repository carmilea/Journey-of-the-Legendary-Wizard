Maureen Olsen walked among the 129 others who had survived the night, moving up from the shore. Her feet moved on their own, the salt dried stiff in her robe.

The Dark Forest was simply a stretch of dense trees, their black branches twisting and turning into strange shapes.

Black trees. New flora, Tally informed her. Please provide a leaf for analysis.

Not now, she thought silently.

The chip obligingly tagged the trees anyway as they walked through them. The dry bark cracked underfoot. No birds sang in the trees overhead as they went deeper into the forest, Richard walking beside her, his eyes on the ground. Lucy and Alice were behind him, holding hands.

"Oh, what a laughable concealment spell," Evans said, standing in the middle of the forest. He chuckled, the sound soft. "It's almost an insult to be expected to fall for this." His hand slowly closed. There was nothing in it, but he gripped the empty air all the same. The air rippled around his hand, the effect like that of a stone being dropped into water. The rings ran out through the trees, distorting the view.

The forest folded away like a curtain being pulled back. A huge castle stood before them, its spires piercing a lid of black cloud that hung over it. It sat in the middle of flat ground, the black trees now a good distance away.

> Warning: visual field altered. Unable to determine nature of force field.

Tally noted. I have recorded the illusion as faithfully as I have the castle ahead. I am unable to determine which is real.

Great, Maureen thought silently.

I assure you, I recorded the illusion with complete accuracy. I could not make it look any different if I wished.

That's not comforting.

It was not intended to be. Tally sounded smug.

Maureen stared up at the castle spires for a moment, feeling a wonder that was not dulled by the massacre of the night before. Then the air began to grow cold and she felt it on her face, rolling off the black stone.

There was a crunch of gritty sound from the statues flanking the gate as the spell was broken and they came to life. Grit sifted down from their joints, and their crimson eyes flared to life and fixed themselves on the seeds. One roared, "Who dares trespass on the Dark Forest?" Maureen's hand slid to the hilt of the Cross Sword beneath her robe.

>Analysing. Comparing to gate. Height, [≈ 5.3 m]. Marking lizard head on man's body. Marking steel trident, length [≈ 2.7 m]. Stone is trembling, but not due to vocal projection. Emotion more akin to fear. Maureen, these stone creatures are afraid.

The lizard-headed statue stared at Evans, then stammered, "L-Lord Evans?" The second statue said nothing, its crimson eyes full of fear.

Evans walked forward, his slit eyes half smiling. "Yes, Hugh, Iman, very dutiful," he said aloud without looking at either statue. Both statues nodded frantically, grit sliding from their joints at the motion. "I will be taking my seeds inside." And he kept walking.

Hugh-or-Iman? Tally asked.

Evans said both names in one breath, and never looked at either of them, Maureen replied silently.

Very well, Tally said, a touch put out. Both statues filed under Hugh-or-Iman.

Very scientific, Maureen noted.

"Even Forest wizards give a password here," Emily muttered just behind Maureen.

Evans continued walking, giving no password. He must be very strong, Maureen decided, to have such standing. She'd never seen anyone accorded so much respect just by their presence.

Evans led the way through the gate, the seeds shuffling along after him. Then, abruptly, he broke into dark particles and was gone, leaving the seeds to shuffle about awkwardly in the courtyard.

It was paved with black flagstone, beaded with moisture from the morning air. It was cold beneath her boots, and smelled strongly of incense, but there was something rotten beneath the scent. A smell of rottenness. Richard stood beside her, his face unusually pale this morning. Lucy and Alice were right behind her, both girls obviously frightened.

No one spoke.

* * *

Emily turned about. "Come, seeds," she said, her voice as cold as the stones beneath their feet. "I will take you to Lord Hegel, so that he may assign you your mentors. Follow me. Do not look around, do not wander. This is no place for you to gawk."

The seeds bunched together in a tight group behind the Dark Forest apprentice, and they walked through the castle corridors. Torches spat in iron brackets. Light danced over the wet walls, the black stone seeming to sweat beneath the humidity. Incense hung in the air, fighting against the smell of mildew and decay that seemed to have seeped into every crevice. Their footsteps sounded strangely loud, echoing off the vaulted ceiling.

Black-robes passed by—apprentices and wizards both, men and women. Occasionally Emily would smile at someone or give them a casual word as they walked by. To others she gave no notice, simply moving to the wall to let them pass and bowing her head until they had gone by.

As one apprentice—the one that Emily smiled at—passed Maureen, the cold voice flashed within her mind. [Warning: Energy radiation source. Withdraw.]

When the first black-robe that Emily bowed her head for had passed—one of the wizard class with his robes pulled up about his face and his hood pulled low—another warning came. [Warning: HIGH energy radiation source. Withdraw immediately.]

Two bands now, Tally said inside her head. Scarlette, Berret, Derrick, and Emily in one band. Evans and Ciri in another. Finally enough samples to draw a line.

So you can grade them now, Maureen answered silently, her eyes fixed on Emily's back.

One line. Nothing finer. But it is a very good line.

Maureen began to understand. The ones Emily treated as equals were apprentices; the ones she bowed her head for were full-fledged wizards.

Eleven for eleven, Maureen laid each alert next to an occasion where Emily bowed her head.

That was good data, Tally said smugly.

Let it think it was clever, Maureen decided. It probably was.

A tall wizard brushed past her, close enough that his black robe sleeve touched her own. He swept on ahead of them, the air in his wake seeming colder than the corridors normally were. Maureen kept her eyes fixed on the floor.

I wonder if this is what it means to be remade by wizard energy, she thought. You walk the same paths that wizards tread, breathing the same air they breathe, until you become a source of the energy yourself.

Energy pollution.

Yes, Maureen, Tally said. I have been reading you for many months now. [Host: mild energy radiation]. That line is moving.

Keep that number there for me, will you? Maureen asked silently. She glanced down at her own fingers, lit by the spitting torches set in iron brackets along the stone walls. They looked like fingers to her. Ordinary fingers.

* * *

Emily knocked softly with the iron knocker, then pushed the door open and stood aside. A white-haired old man sat inside at a table, yawning in boredom. The papers on the table shifted in drifts as he moved. Jars holding murky things clustered along the shelves of the room. Dust danced in the grey light from the single window. Emily bowed her head. “Lord Hegel, the First-Tier Wizard brought these seeds along.”

Hegel shrugged. “Unlucky little ones. Must have died by the hundreds before this lot got here.”

Emily said nothing more, just bowed again and hurried out of the room.

[Warning: HIGH energy radiation source.] flashed on Maureen’s display as she looked up at Hegel.

Twelve for twelve, Tally said smugly.

Stop counting your wins, she told it silently.

129 out of 547, she thought. She kept her hands perfectly still at her sides.

Hegel rose from his chair and stretched. “No need to be nervous. There are two ways you can choose your mentor. I have a cross-star orb here that will make the choice for you if you like. Or you can pay a fee and make your own choice.” He held out an open palm, and a rhombus-shaped crystal spun up out of it. As it turned, it grew larger, until it hovered above the table, faintly glowing.

Tally spoke up. The shape matches the Sunset Canyon test crystal. And the way it spins matches, too.

Maureen felt the old cold creep into her fingers, making them curl slightly.

Fingers, Maureen. You have fingers.

A seed spoke up. “Gold or gems will do?”

Hegel raised one finger. “Gold coins? Gems? Who wants those? For wizards, magic stones are the only currency.”

Maureen’s hand went to the pouch tied around her chest, where Derrick’s stones and the ones taken from the seed who had ambushed her lay hidden. Pay, then. Don’t trust that draw.

The pouch has never been counted, Tally said in its own voice. The twenty-three is just what showed at the mouth in the dark.

Maureen took several steps back until she was standing behind the group of seeds. She turned her shoulder away from the room, keeping the front of her body in shadow thanks to the voluminous robe. Then she tipped out the contents of the pouch onto her hand, the Seal staying safely in its inner pocket.

[Magic stones: 109 + 5 = 114]

You miscounted last time, she thought silently.

No. I was counting exactly what you showed me.

The stones were cool on her skin, giving off a faint glow. Maureen calmly poured them back into her pouch, her thumb sliding through the silk and counting, as imperturbable as any gambler knowing he held a winning hand.

The seeds went up one by one and Hegel assigned each their mentor. The nervous boy, whom Hegel referred to as Iss when he drew, was told his mentor was someone called Vivian. “A very beautiful lady,” Hegel added with a grin. He clapped his hands and there was a boom and a shadow servant was standing there. Its outline was blurred, more like a smear of smoke on glass than anything solid. It took Maureen a second glance before she could even see its edges, and another few seconds before she could see it at all.

Hegel spoke kindly to the young man, who stammered a thank you before following the shadow servant from the room. Another seed, a big boy, gave his name and drew, as did most of the others. Only Anna and a couple other unexpected strangers paid for the privilege of choosing their own mentors.

The crowd was thinning by the time Rheas walked forward. “I would like to choose my mentor,” he said. “But I don’t have enough to pay right now. I’ll pay double once I’m a wizard, though. You’ll not regret it, I assure you. I have a high aptitude.”

Tally flagged the set of Rheas’s jaw muscles and the blond youth who stood beside him, staring intently at the floor.

“Ah, indeed?” Hegel laughed. “You do? Well, that is admirable. But rules are rules. They can’t be changed.” Rheas frowned, but put his hand on the crystal. The rest, with similar hopes to be able to pay later, gave up.

Now it was her turn. “I will pay,” Maureen said, her voice calm and emotionless.

“Really?” Hegel peered at her suspiciously. “That’s surprising. Let me check.” He bent over, riffling through a pile of books before plucking one out and dropping it onto the table with a thud. “Every mentor in the Dark Forest, eh?”

He eyed her for a moment, then grinned slyly. “Very well, one magic stone per quarter hour.”

Maureen paused a beat before nodding.

Rules are rules, Tally noted. They apply to us as well. [00:00 / 15:00] How fast can you turn pages?
