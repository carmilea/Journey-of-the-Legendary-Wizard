The horse blew twin plumes of steam from its nostrils as it pulled the cart along the iron-hardened roadway. It shook its head, making all the brass fittings on its harness jingle. In this stark grey land, the noise was almost the only sign of life for miles around. Maureen sat on the driver’s bench with the reins slack across her gloved hands. Every so often she would turn and look back at what was concealed beneath the oilcloth sheeting. When she did so, a small smile would appear and disappear. One wheel jolted in a rut and glass chimed beneath the oilcloth, prompting her hand to swing back over the seat without her conscious thought. She pressed down on the crate of burners and flasks, packed in straw, the items she could never afford to replace. Beside it was a wooden box, the contents padded in straw as well, the grenades that she had made.

[Grenades: 6]

*You steadied the grenades before the glass,* it pointed out.

*They don’t explode when they break,* she told it silently.

*I counted them anyway, twice. That’s how much I care.*

Maureen smiled, just a little.

She had stones enough for once. The crates of potions could be sold off piecemeal, along the road. A vial left on a shelf too long would go cloudy. Cloudy was money turning into water. Jing Sheng had despised wasted reagents, and Maureen Olsen was no different. The grenades, though . . . those she would keep making.

*How many is enough?* Tally asked.

*At my stage?* she answered silently. *There is no such thing as too many.*

*I’ll hold you to that. Stage.*

The cold air bit deep into the inside of her nose, making it sting, and it had a taste—earth, horse, and iron from the tyres. A few clumps of scrubby brush passed by, grey and naked, with white rime on the side the wind had found. Nothing moved in the scrub save for a crow that rose into the low white sky.

*Tally, give me an honest line on myself. No kindness, no evasion. Just the facts.*

[Maureen Olsen: strong Third-Class Wizard Apprentice. Not exceptional. The optimised models grant edge within her rank, not step above.]

*Yes. I know.*

*Nobody walks the Passise without something up their sleeve.*

The wagon rolled east, creaking gently, its iron tyres crunching the frozen rime in the tracks left by other vehicles. The horse’s breath steamed around its head, and the scrub rolled past, unremarkable. Tally was still going on about her trumps. *Dark Wind Ring is a trump,* Tally said. *Not a guarantee.*

[Seal: glow near dark]

*As it should be.*

*You know my view,* Tally said more gently.

*Pocket.*

*Pocket,* Tally agreed, sounding relieved.

Still Maureen’s hand drifted to her inner pocket. The Seal rested coldly against her ribs, the cloth of her travelling robe between them insufficient to keep out all trace of its chill. The place behind her ribs remembered the dull ache that always came with use of the artefact. An ache like a bruise, one that had learned her name.

*Which leaves,* Tally said, brightening, *the grenades.*

*Of course. Tally’s favourite column.*

*It likes anything that goes bang,* Maureen teased it silently.

*Anything that keeps you alive,* Tally answered. *Bang is a bonus.*

The recall sat in her guts like a swallowed stone. The scroll had burned with a black flame in mid-air, the words dancing before her eyes. Augurit’s voice, old and dry and unhurried, promising a thousand years of soulfire for deserters. The brand on her wrist prickled once at the memory, as though Augurit’s words had been written there with a hot iron instead of spoken.

[Recall: Day 29.]

*It would take approximately twenty days to travel straight to the Dark Forest on this road,* Tally said. *I hear you planning something with the other fifty.*

*Workshop.*

*Ah.*

So she would go slowly. She would visit every known wizard gathering place along the long way to the Dark Forest. She would sell potions, buy Hillan Metal, and build shells. Turning the Steward’s death threat into a workshop schedule made her laugh once, sharp and brief, into her scarf. A crow rose from the grey scrub brush and flapped up into the low white sky.

* * *

*Blake. Rose. Ambergris.*

She had not asked for it, but Tally spoke up anyway. *Four layers. I don’t have a nose,* it reminded her, *but I can smell what you smell. What you smell is Blake.* A particle trace came along with the scents, invisible, at Second-Class density. *Behind. To the left.*

She did not turn her head. She pulled back on the reins until the wagon creaked to a halt. Then she spoke, not loudly. “Blake. Come out.”

There was no answer. Somewhere in the thorny scrub behind them to the left a drop of frost water fell from the end of a twig, ticking like a clock. The horse blew out an exasperated breath, shifted its hooves in the muddy ruts, and shook its head. The warm, sweet scents of Blake’s rose-and-ambergris perfume lingered in the still air, thick and cloying. This was how he smelled when he was holding his breath behind a bush.

*He’s choosing the hard way,* Tally told her.

> Amra-Keis. Low. Back.

Maureen opened her hand. “Amra-Keis.” The word was barely out of her mouth before a ball of boiling green acid formed over her palm, bubbling white as it smoked in the cold. She flung it hard into the scrub brush off to her left, a hand’s width short of the particle trace that hung in the air.

The acid ball hit with a hiss that sounded like fat dropped onto a griddle. The ground bubbled up, then sank down again, leaving a pitted crater in the dirt. Thumb-size droplets of green acid slid off the dying thornbushes, turning the plants to a slime of limp, dripping leaves. A sour stink like burnt metal rolled across the road, and the horse tossed its head in alarm.

Ahead of the spreading edge of the acid, Blake tumbled up and out of the ditch, scrambling up to his feet. His good coat was smeared with mud from shoulder to knee. A twig from a thornbush was caught in his carefully trimmed beard. His face went grey, then flushed a dull, angry red.

Maureen smiled at him, but there was no welcome in her eyes. “Blake. You’ve been on my heels since the city gate. I doubt you came all this way just to wave me good-bye.”

He took a deep breath, then mumbled, “I’m sorry, really, I am...” before it all burst out of him. “Where’s Bina? She went to Grey Dove with you. You came back. She didn’t. Why didn’t she come back?”

Maureen raised one eyebrow, letting her hand come to rest lightly against the wagon’s wooden rail. “We parted ways outside Grey Dove. She had business there. She didn’t tell me what it was.”

“Liar!” His voice cracked like a whip. “I asked Albert to ask you. He turned as white as lard and said no. They all said no. What did they see? Tell me where she is, Maureen. Tell me!” He took another step closer, chin tucked into his chest, weight forward on the balls of his feet.

*That question is the sum total of him,* Maureen thought. *What did they see? He wasn’t on that trip with us. He doesn’t know what I can do. He’s never seen me fight, so he came here to demand an answer to a fear he doesn’t even know he has. Albert kept his mouth shut. If only Blake had done the same.*

Tally highlighted the pulse hammering away at the side of Blake’s throat. *He’s frightened of you,* Tally said quietly. *And he doesn’t know why.*

*Then he should listen to it.*

“You’d cross another wizard for a woman who was never yours?” Maureen’s voice dropped in pitch and cooled by several degrees. “Has mooning over a woman addled your brain, Blake?”

There was very little patience left in her tone.

Blake’s face twisted in anger. “Fine. I’ll make you talk. I was casting spells before you were a seed in your mother’s womb.” His lips began moving through the first line of a long, rambling chant.

> He’s chanting. Long form.
> Window: 3 s.

*Enough.*

Maureen thought, and she moved.

Her hand dropped down toward the thorn scrub growing next to Blake. As she reached out to the dark particles within, the cost began flowing out of her arrays and down her arm. It felt like cold water running down the inside of a sleeve. Her fingertips went numb with the chill. The shadow beneath the thornbush deepened, then stood up behind Blake.

He never made it to the second line of his spell. A great, misshapen hand of shadow rose soundlessly from the scrub at his side, closing once in midair before plunging straight through his belly and ripping out the other side.

There was no other noise on the road but the wet crackle of corrosion. The silk and flesh around the hole turned black and slumped inward, the edges creeping wider. The stench of rotting vegetation came up through the rose-and-ambergris scent, like the door to a cellar slamming open.

Blake looked down at himself, then up at her, his lips white. “The Hand of Negative Energy . . . it can’t be that strong . . . Maureen, when did you—”

Tally finished the question for her ears alone. *Third-Class, optimised.*

He hadn’t known she had it. No one in Wintercold had.

Maureen Olsen turned to face him, giving him one glance. “How sad. Should I call you naive, Blake, or just stupid?”

A deep-red Fireball swelled on her palm, then she threw it. There was a roar from the blast, a slap of heat across her cheeks. The horse shied in its traces. When the light died, there was only a scattering of black ash across the mud. The ash began to lift in the west wind, a scent of burnt rose hanging faintly in the air.

[Mana: 7.8 → ~5.2.]

*Ash only,* Tally reported. *No sign of anyone else on the road for two kilometres. By sight.*

*Albert will wonder,* Maureen said silently.

*Albert already wonders,* Tally answered. *Now he’ll stop asking.*

*Your pulse didn’t move once. Start to finish. Same as with Lauren. Writing that down for both of us.*

Maureen gave no answer.

A fleck of black ash had landed on the back of her glove. She brushed at it, but it smeared in a grey streak instead of falling away. She rubbed it with her thumb, polishing the leather up and down until it was clean. It took a good deal more work than it should have.

*He’d just wanted to know where she was.*

Maureen said it to herself once, just to see if it still hurt. It didn’t, not anymore. She noted that as well, then turned her attention to soothing the trembling horse with a touch of her hand to its neck before driving forward past the still-smoking crater.

* * *

At the edge of the first settlement, she caught sight of a stranger reflected in a dark shop window. His face was plain, a young man with mousy brown hair, a weak jaw, and a travel-worn grey coat. She’d made use of her old sellsword illusion again, with a few modifications.

She tried one word, under her breath. “Hello,” she said.

Her voice answered from his lips, a girl’s voice. So much for that.

>Disguise: holding.

>Mana drain: 0.1 / hour.

The face needed a name, though. She reached out, feeling for something appropriate, and the name ‘Richard’ rose unbidden to her mind.

*Sentimental,* Tally said.

*Familiar,* she answered silently.

*Both,* Tally said. *And he’s got your voice too. I’ll count words spoken using this disguise.*

That was good. Fewest words won.

The name felt strange on her tongue. Richard calling her a block of wood during drill. Richard laughing too loudly at the stream. A name that meant home, worn over a stranger’s face. It left a small ache under her breastbone, but she didn’t feel the need to mention it to Tally.

Weeks later, Maureen walked the crooked lanes of another settlement cold and damp beneath a dry-looking illusion. The lamps gleamed past like drowned moons in the fog.

The big-nosed shopkeeper held her Berserk Blood Potion up to his magnifying lens for a long time before speaking. “Hm. Hmm. Where’d a lad like you get something like this? No, don’t tell me, better if you don’t tell me. Thirty years I been appraising potions, Mr. Richard. I’ve had maybe four this clean run through my hands. Four.”

He had a nose like a ripe plum, enormous and red-veined. His breath fogged the lens as he leaned in close, peering at the ruby liquid within. The potion glimmered in the light of his shop lamp, casting a stain of dark red light across the scarred countertop between them.

Tally noted the jump in his pulse at his throat the moment he’d unstoppered the vial and sniffed. That told Maureen he would pay more than what he was about to swear he wouldn’t.

She raised her eyes to meet his and spoke in a low, flat voice. “Five hundred.”

The big-nosed shopkeep groaned as if she’d trod upon his foot. “Five hun... you’ll have the shirt off my back! Four-twenty, and I’m being soft.”

Maureen Olsen said nothing at all. Instead she rested one finger lightly on the vial and drew it back across the counter an inch.

*Two words so far,* Tally told her.

She was winning. Tally tracked the shopkeep’s eyes, seeing how they followed the vial. *He is going to say yes.*

“Blunt, aren’t you. Blunt as a brick.” The shopkeeper rubbed his nose, then sighed deeply. “Fine. Five hundred, and I’m robbing myself.” He counted out the stones into a black silk pouch, doing so slowly as if each one pained him to let go. As Maureen reached for the pouch, he leaned in close. “Stay for a cup, Mr. Richard? Which house d’you brew for, eh? A lad with hands like yours . . .”

“My own,” Maureen said. “Good day.” She had slipped out the door before he could get the kettle off its hook.

*Six words,* Tally said. *A record.*

In the lane outside, the weighty pouch resting in her palm and clicking with each step, Tally alerted Maureen to three men detaching themselves from the wall at the corner up ahead, peeling away like strips of wet paper. *Wizard hyenas,* it said. *They prey upon lone travellers.*

The plain young man Maureen Olsen was pretending to be looked just like a lone traveller.

Maureen walked them out past the fog’s edge into the wet scrub beyond. Here, no rules of any settlement applied. Before the first wizard hyena’s knife cleared its sheath, the Cross Sword was buried in his wrist. The rest of them took even less time than the haggling had.

Maureen wiped the blade on the wet grass beside the body. One of the three men wore a brooch—cheap copper, shaped like a dog’s head—which she left in the mud. This road had shown her both kindness and malice; families had courted her and families had sneered at her. These three men were just rubbish.

She noticed that Tally had kept the count running off her display, just as it had with Overboard and Remains. She asked once, *How many on this road?*

*Seven,* Tally told her. *You asked.*

Then the number was gone from her vision again.

* * *

At the last settlement before she entered the Dark Forest, Maureen had sold the wagon and the horse. It was a fool’s idea to try to roll a creaking wooden wagon past wizard patrols, so she’d sold both to a dealer for what he would pay. He’d led the animal away, and once it had turned its head back to look at her. It was the same horse that had shied away when she had fired off a fireball.

*That had cost you more than it earned,* Tally noted.

*Log it as sound business then,* she told it silently.

*Logged as sound business.*

*Disputed.*

Maureen had carried everything up the black hillside herself, her back protesting at the weight of each crate of clinking grenades, wrapped in padding. The potion stock, the bronze book, and the Sodara trunk were all strapped to her back. The trunk had been slung across her shoulders like a felled sapling, wrapped in a piece of black cloth, and its straps had bitten into her flesh, leaving red lines in her collarbones.

Tonight, Maureen’s shadow danced up the wall of the shallow cave where she stayed. She’d sliced the top off a boulder with the Cross Sword to make herself a table. Its flat face still bore the long strokes of the sword, and flecks of Hillan glinted off its surface like mica. The last group of black shells sat in a row on top of the makeshift table.

Before she started the chant, Tally flagged the leftmost shell. One of the silver rune lines in the upper left of the shell had been drawn shallowly by Maureen’s tired hand just before midnight.

*It will not take the fire evenly,* Tally noted. *It may crack.*

*Like shell 41?* Maureen asked silently.

*Yes, like shell 41,* Tally responded, using the special tone it reserved for a particularly famous patient.

*You’ll bring up shell 41 at my funeral,* she told it.

*Only in the eulogy.*

Maureen used the tip of the Cross Sword to grind out the weak line, then traced it again. This time she did it slowly, pressing deeply into the shell. The warm metal bit into her burned fingertips. She kept drawing until Tally showed her the line glowing as brightly and evenly as the others.

Her lips moved steadily as she chanted the casting enchantment, while her hands formed sign after sign in the air. Then came the moment when she had to shout the Ancient Andas line out loud, projecting her voice into the cave.

“Flame that is everywhere,

“I give you life.

“Answer my summons from this day,

“And give your strength to me!”

Something crackled.

The shells shivered on the stone, and an invisible fire wrapped around them. The silver runes drank deep, until there was nothing left. They dimmed, then were gone. The iron lumps lay on the table again, just so much junk that anyone would kick off the road if they found it lying there.

Maureen picked up one of the grenades, feeling it still faintly warm in her hand. She smiled. “There.”

The flames had not produced any light, only heat. It pressed against her face like an open oven door. The air above the table had wavered with the intensity of the fire’s warmth, making even the far wall seem to ripple. Now the cave smelled like hot metal mixed with the smoky scent of burning resin from the fire.

The pads of Maureen’s fingertips were shiny and burned from touching the hot shells. Stone dust was packed into every crease and divot of her knuckles. When she pushed herself away from the table and stood up, her back cracked like a dry branch being stepped on. What once took her ten days at the fief now took her a single evening.

*That is because the charging chant draws upon your spirit,* Tally told her. *The dragging sensation in your thoughts is simple fatigue. Your body requires rest before anything else.*

*Later,* Maureen told it silently.

*You always say that. Later never comes.*

She sighed, but forced herself to look at the ledger. [Grenades: 71. Magic stones, loose: ~3,100. Card: 950. Recall: Day 76.]

The number in the magic stones column made her wince. She’d left Wintercold richer than she’d ever been in her life.

*I bought safety,* she told Tally silently.

*At a terrible rate.*

But these seventy-one iron lumps were what allowed her to walk back into the Dark Forest. Her plan was to sneak past the patrol wizards and head directly to Gregory’s residence. For that, she pulled out the test tube of grey powder from her sleeve. She’d brewed it for just this purpose.

*I have only one sample of what a full Wizard can notice,* Tally told her. *Adrian reading your rank across a room, with barely a glance.*

*And you don’t like even that one sample,* Maureen noted.

*Wartime patrols: no data.*

Maureen remembered Evans, dissolving himself on the ship into dark particles, willing himself to simply be elsewhere. The tube clicked against her burned thumbnail. Cold settled into her stomach, sitting low and heavy. No amount of time spent in front of the fire could shift it away.

* * *

The grass beneath her feet ran green and wet until it stopped at an invisible line, sharp as if cut by a knife. Beyond it stood the Dark Forest. The trees were black and knotted and dry. There was no birdsong in the boughs, no sound at all. The hairs on her arms stood on end, as though something hidden behind the trees had turned to look at her.

Maureen knelt in the tall grass, the heavy packs pulling at her shoulders. She drew the tube from her sleeve, holding it carefully. The powder within had never been tested against anything but her own senses. If an actual Wizard could smell it...

The glass was cold against her fingers. When she tilted it, the powder slid down the sides like grey ash. The old cold returned to her stomach, the same cold she’d felt standing at the ship’s railing that first day she’d seen this bleak black coast.

Tally began counting off her breaths, dropping them into the rhythm of the Silver Light. [Recall: Day 78]

*Twenty-two days early,* Tally noted. *Earliest returner on file.*

*I’m not returning,* Maureen thought back silently. *I’m sneaking.*

*The file cannot tell the difference yet,* Tally responded.

Maureen’s thumb rested against the stopper when something moved at the very top of her vision. A great shape dropped away from the black crag above the treeline with a single, heavy leap.

*Known. Hugh. Large. Smiling. That’s worse.*

The ground shuddered beneath her knees as the living statue landed. Wet green grass flattened into a circle around it. The scent of cold stone and ancient spit filled the air.

A huge lizard head swung down to hers, a steel trident planted beside it in the soft earth.

The jaws opened, revealing rows of sharp teeth that glinted coldly.

“H-hey! Young w-wizard apprentice, h-hello there!” The head tilted, delighted with its greeting. “You’re b-back?”

A long, scarlet tongue slopped out and back between those teeth. A drop of spittle fell beside her boot, making the wet grass hiss and smoke.

Maureen’s stomach sank like a stone dropped down a well.

*Hugh used to stand at the gate,* Tally said dryly. *But now the war has it walking the fence.*

*So much for sneaking,* Maureen thought.

Maureen closed her fist over the stoppered tube, then slid it back into her sleeve. She smiled up into all those sharp teeth until her cheeks hurt from the effort.

“Ho ho! Oh ho ho!”

Hugh’s laughter rumbled over the wet grass.

“W-welcome back to the D-Dark Forest!”
