No one spoke near the wall where Frank was crumpled like a pile of old laundry. His broken hand lay bent across his chest in the wrong direction. The onlookers had not expected Maureen to drop the loudest Second-Class apprentice in the arcade with three moves.

*Every pair of feet inside the ring took a half pace back,* Tally told Maureen. *Your breathing has not changed one bit. I counted.*

*I saw,* Maureen said silently.

*I just wanted to be thorough.*

“It’s over, Richard. Let’s go.”

Maureen said it with no inflection, sweeping the circle of faces with her eyes once.

Richard nodded dumbly, like a man who had just taken a blow on the head. Lucy came with them, both hands clutching the back of Richard’s sleeve.

The black robes rustled apart to let them through. Someone began to whistle a low note, then swallowed it. Maureen looked around her and not one face met her eyes. She found that she actually preferred their silence to the cheering that she had been half prepared for.

Once seated in the dining hall, nestled on the long bench, a silver knife in her hand, Maureen came straight to the point.

“What happened back there, Richard? Are you in trouble?”

“Trouble? Me? Oh, no! Nothing like that. Just little things.” He tugged his sleeve down to meet the cuff of his robe.

Lucy’s fingers crept up to find the hem of Richard’s sleeve. They gave it one tug, as if to say, Tell her.

Richard pulled his arm off the table, placing it in his lap, out of Lucy’s reach. The flaxen-haired girl folded her hands in her lap and stared hard at her plate.

Richard noticed Maureen watching Lucy and hurriedly filled up the suddenly awkward gap in the conversation. “I said ‘little’ things. You know how it is. There are bumps on a wizard’s road. We all get them. I get them. No big deal. Honestly, no big deal.”

 His collar was frayed, his robe hemmed twice over by a clumsy hand. His nails were bitten to the quick. It reminded Maureen that Vivian didn’t teach much. *He owes more than he’s saying,* Tally told her. The Skran second son cut his meat into small pieces, eating the bread first. When he thought no one was looking, he slid a roll into his sleeve, as the children used to do in the slums. Maureen felt her throat tighten. She remembered him on the road, pushing his snack bag across to her without a second thought. Now the young master Skran would rather starve than take help from the girl who used to fetch his boots for him. Middle aptitude was as common in the Forest as gravel on a path. Lucy was low aptitude.

*He won’t take help from me,* she told Tally silently.

*He’ll take a gift,* said Tally. *Tell him it’s a gift.*

She leaned in under the low murmur of the hall. “If Frank has friends, and he looks like the type who’d borrow them, tell them your friend is Third-Class.”

A pause. “That’s me. Don’t pass it around at parties.” Maureen heard Lucy catch her breath, and felt Richard stare at her.

*That cost you something you kept hidden for months,* Tally noted.

*It buys him something he couldn’t buy for himself,* Maureen answered silently.

*Logged as a trade,* said Tally. *Not a good one.*

*A kind one.*

*Those are rarely good ones.*

Maureen lifted her stemmed glass towards the two of them, and her face relaxed. “To you two. It took you long enough.”

Richard turned a bright red that extended to the tips of his ears, while Lucy hid a smile behind her hand.

His voice had climbed half a tone in her presence since Sunset Canyon. The night Kelly died, Maureen had seen the flaxen-haired girl lean into Richard’s arms. Now, whatever was going on between them had arrived somewhere. She was glad, with one small ache mixed in with her gladness. After the realm, none of them would be these people again. Gladness wanted to give them something to hold in their hands.

So Maureen set a box carved from dark wood on the table and slid it across the rough boards towards them. It scraped loudly in the quiet dining hall, and there was a clink from inside as one vial bounced against the straw packing.

“My gift,” she said. “For the pair of you.”

Lucy glanced at Richard before she lifted the lid. Inside were fourteen stoppered vials nestled in straw, and three of them glowed with a sullen orange light that shone up into the girl’s face. Her hand hovered over the glowing bottles, unwilling to touch them. She drew a sharp breath through her teeth.

*Gift box opened: Berserk Blood 5. Stamina 6. Incendiary 3. Total 14,* Tally said as the lid rose. It paused for a moment. *Incendiaries are first batch. Formula recorded in Gregory’s library. Attack potions are rare. Most apprentices never see them except on a top shelf.*

Richard’s face drained of colour. “Maureen, I— no. No, I can’t, you— those are—” He pushed the box back an inch with two fingers, as if he thought it might bite him. “Thank you, but, no.”

She kept her smile gentle and made no move to take the box back. “It’s a gift. That’s all it is.” Then, very softly, “Would you turn down a gift, Richard? From me?”

She knew what she was doing to him. It had cost her several evenings bent over the burner. It would keep the two of them alive for at least the season.

But it would sting his pride to accept it.

She pushed again anyway. Pride healed. The dead did not.

She watched the gift go in like a splinter.

At last, he took it with both hands, cradling it the way a servant would accept something from a lady. “Thank you, Maureen,” he said, too softly. Then he rallied. “Vivian’s had us copying the same frog for three weeks. Lucy does better frogs than I do.”

Lucy laughed, one beat late.

After that, each sentence sat up a little straighter than the one before. Once, Richard even said my lady and blushed scarlet to the tips of his ears. *You are Lady again,* Tally said quietly.

*I noticed,* she responded.

Tally sighed. *Sorry. I know you wanted to be Maureen.*

She let out a long breath through her nose without making a sound. For once, Tally had nothing to say.

They finished their meal quickly and stood. As they rose, she noticed the empty end of the bench where Aaron had been sitting on the day of the reunion. He’d sat with his back to the wall.

“Richard. Where’s Aaron? I haven’t seen him.”

“Aaron?” Richard scratched the back of his neck. “Haven’t seen him in . . . months? One of the apprentices in Vivian’s hall swears he went out on a job and never came back. Dead out there, they’re saying. No one’s heard a word.”

She froze, the knife resting on her plate. Sulphur rose in her memory, as if it were being cooked out of the meat that lay before her. The serpent’s cave in the rubble. A forge-bellows breath beneath a mountain peak.

>sulphur on Aaron at the rubble

>phantom serpent below the peak

>realm's Black Mando record, close but not certain

*Dead is one hypothesis,* Tally said. *I have no data on it.*

*And the other?*

*No data on that either. I just like it better.*

“Is that so,” she said, nodding slowly. They left with the box between them, and Lucy looked back once as they reached the door.

* * *

She walked the meal off through the corridors, and the sulphur stayed in her nose all the way down. Tally kept count of the turns as it always did.

The noise from the Potion Apprentices’ Gathering reached her ears before she got to the nineteenth corridor. The noise of the marketplace was louder and rawer than she remembered.

Behind George’s stall the question cloth was alive, scuttling with lines of energy particles in frantic motion: fastest antidote for unknown venom, healing potions wanted, any price, any price.

“Ah, welcome, friend, you’ve come to exactly the right—Maureen? You’re Maureen! Where did you crawl out from?”

George’s voice cracked over the stalls of jars and pickled roots.

His freckles stood out against his pale face. “I thought you were dead.”

He still had brine on his sleeves, and his smile still threatened to crack his face. After Richard’s my lady, being shouted at like any other customer at a stall was enough to loosen something tight under Maureen’s ribs.

“Good to see you too, George,” she said. “I want some Hillan metal.”

George bloomed like a flower, his face splitting into a grin.

“Hillan! Why, come in, come in. Isn’t that lucky, Maureen, isn’t that lucky. Lucky you’re alive.”

*Lucky for you,* Maureen thought. *A dead woman can’t spend money at your stall.* She laughed aloud at her own joke.

*That one has been filed under your worst jokes. I’m surprised you found it amusing.*

George drew out a box of Hillan and set it on his stall. The pieces glittered like fish scales in the lamplight, cold against her palm and heavier than they looked.

She picked out the dullest piece and weighed it in her hand. Tally checked its grain against the small lot Albert had sold her in Wintercold. No dead patches. [Hillan: est. 24 shells]. *You are not being cheated by George this time. That is almost suspicious.*

“All of it.”

George blinked. “You want all this? Of course, of course!” He rattled off a price, rubbing his hands together. “That’ll be one hundred and seventy stones.”

“Not in stones,” Maureen said. “Finished potions.”

The potion apprentice blinked again, then his grin widened. “Potions? Of course! Of course!”

*His grin got brighter instead of dimmer,* Tally pointed out. *That’s not how people look when they’re getting cheated.*

*I know. And that’s not the expression of a man who’s just received a fairer deal than the one he asked for.*

The reason became apparent as soon as she left the Potion Apprentices’ Gathering and entered the Trading District proper. A thin boy walked up to a stall, examined the wares, and traded a fine dagger with a silver chased hilt for a single vial of Recovery. Then he clutched the phial to his chest and hurried away, like it was his own beating heart.

Three stalls down from him, a materials seller was erasing the chalk numbers on his slate with a damp cloth, scribbling new numbers below. Everyone knew the trial was coming.

[Finished potions: +60–110%]

[Raw materials: −30–50%]

*Pay in potions, buy with stones, brew, sell.*

*Very merchantlike, Tally,* she said silently.

*I had a good teacher in Gandolf,* it replied, sounding just the tiniest bit smug. *Even posthumously.*

Maureen had George’s Hillan packed up in a sack before moving on to the herb stalls. There, she picked up dried Phoenix Orchid Root for half of last month’s price, and bought a large bundle of Star-vein Leaf Grass. With those ingredients, she could brew enough Recovery and Incendiary to sell into the rush. A second sack held odds and ends whose names only lived in Tally’s ledger.

*The storm really is coming,* Maureen thought, shifting the sacks on her shoulder as she made her way through the hoarse-throated crowd.

* * *

The lights came on by themselves as Maureen sat down. The racks were the same width apart as they had been on Jing Sheng’s white bench, where there had been tweezers and gloves. Here there were bare scarred hands, rows of dull black shells, and a chant in a dead tongue.

Maureen shaped the casting gestures, and murmured the Ancient Andas line for charging the grenade with fire.

There was a thin hiss from the room, as flame that no eye could see was drawn down and consumed whole within the iron.

Silver runes rose to the surface of the black shell, gleaming and dancing in the air for just an instant before being swallowed back into the iron. The room filled with the smell of hot metal, and Maureen felt her fingertips blister again, in exactly the same spots as the time before.

Tally timed each charge, and announced its findings. Based upon Maureen’s breathing, and the pulse that beat loud in her ears, it inferred that this was the same amount of spirit that had dragged her down to 7.4.

*I know,* Maureen said silently. *Yes, I do know. I was there too, remember?*

*I will continue to make note of it,* Tally replied, *so that you know that I know you know.*

Maureen rolled the completed grenade in the palm of her hand, feeling the wizard energy throbbing faintly within. “Good,” she said. One corner of her mouth twisted up.

[Grenades: 71 → 95. Hillan: 0.]

*George’s box is gone,* Tally reminded her.

*We’ll get more,* she told it.

*That is your answer for everything,* Tally said.

*More is the right answer here,* Maureen replied.

She packed the grenades away in their padded trays. Small and round, black with silver runes, a war in a drawer. Hillan dust glittered on Maureen’s blistered fingertips.

*Adrian’s summons arrived three hours and eleven minutes ago,* Tally said.

*I lost track,* Maureen admitted.

*That is why I count.*

She wrapped a strip of linen around her fingers before leaving, the black stone of the corridor cool beneath her hand.

* * *

New pots crowded among the glassware racks that lined the long stone bench. Tiny plants sprouted in each, clipped close by expert hands, the floor scattered with the snipped green clippings. The burner ticked on its stone stand, the heatless black flame wavering like a candle in a breeze. The sharp chemical smell of the lab was muted by the fresh scent of crushed sap.

“Mentor.”

Maureen bowed her head as she entered, then saw who stood at his side.

“Winnie.”

She smiled at Adrian’s senior apprentice, the foundling he had raised. Winnie nodded back, staying where she was.

Winnie had not hugged her. *Winnie hugs everyone,* Tally noted.

*Tally, be quiet about that,* Maureen told it silently. *Just note it.*

Adrian put down his pruning shears and didn’t quite look at her. “Olsen. You’re off to that trial.” He snipped one more leaf from the plant. “I took this out of my stock. Don’t make a fuss about it.”

He held out a paper twist, the edges crackling between his wrinkled fingers. Even folded, the sharp scent escaped. The black flame of the burner glinted off the lenses of Adrian’s glasses as he turned back to the plant before Maureen could read his expression.

“Thank you, Mentor.”

Caught by surprise, Maureen bowed her head even lower than before.

“Mm.” Adrian spoke to the leaves. “Go on. And come back. Those grubs won’t clean themselves.”

*The smell was sharp enough to cut through paper,* Tally said. *We should give it a proper look when we get home.*

*Wait for now,* Maureen told Tally silently.

The door closed behind them and the tick-tick-tick of the burner faded away to nothing. The corridor was cold after the heat of Adrian’s laboratory. Their footsteps seemed loud on the stone flags.

Winnie walked beside Maureen. Her face was soft and sad. “I never thought the Dark Forest would come to this. A trial. How many of you will come back?”

“None of us wants it,” Maureen said with a small smile. “But there’s nothing any of us can do.”

“Be careful, Maureen. Prepare properly. I— our mentor, he—” Winnie’s fingers pinched at the same fold in her white gauze dress, over and over, until the cloth creased. She half stepped backward, toward Adrian’s door. “If you need anything, tell me. Whatever I can do for you, I will.”

Tally catalogued the tells for Maureen’s benefit: an apology in Winnie’s voice, the repeatedly creased fold in her gown, the half step backward toward the door. On Maureen’s display, Tally noted:

> Guilt, by sight.
> Cause: no data.

*Winnie has always been kind to me,* Maureen answered silently.

Tally replied, *And this is exactly why the guilt is showing on Winnie.*

Winnie meant every word. She was sorry for something, but Maureen couldn’t figure out what.

Maureen nodded, agreeing to Winnie’s offer. Winnie turned and went back into Adrian’s laboratory, leaving behind the faint scent of crushed herbs and lye soap hanging in the cold corridor air.

* * *

Maureen sat at her desk and unfolded the twist. A black-brown powder fell out from the paper. The powder was fine like soot. It had a smell that stung the back of her nose, and made her eyes water.

*Soot? Is this . . . ?* Maureen asked silently.

> Scentless Bug powder.

Tally matched up the colour, the grainy texture, and the peculiar sting that made Maureen’s nose wrinkle against the dust Daniel had brushed onto her sleeve in Olit’s shop. The powder had followed her everywhere she had gone. According to the library entry, the dust could strip away a scent and hide the trail of a traveler.

Tally continued, *But there is more. The library does not mention this, but I have found that a mother bug can sense the powder’s presence. Adrian meant for you to use it to hide yourself, but it may also be used to mark you.*

*So that’s Adrian’s wish. Hide. Sit it out. Go home when it’s safe.* She laughed softly as she looked at the dust. “Mentor, you really are . . .”

Then Tally presented a plan on her display, showing the lines one after another. *This is what you can do with the powder,* it said. Its own idea.

Maureen read the plan twice, and her lips parted.

“It can actually be used like this?” she said aloud to the empty room. Then she laughed. It was a real laugh, full of mirth and surprise, and it echoed off the stone walls.

*I’ve been saving that since Olit’s shop,* Tally said.

*Stop being so smug,* Maureen told it silently.

*I’m being precise. Isn’t that what you want?*

The next evening, Adrian extinguished the burner and stood Maureen in the corner among the glass pots. In the darkness, the lab smelled of tree sap, cold glass, and the soap from Maureen’s skin.

“Lye,” he said from across the bench. “I could find you blindfolded in a dark room. Still, you’re not hidden. Breathe through your mouth. Most of the dead in these things moved first.”

Night after night he went on like that, and Maureen washed in plain water from then on.

Between lessons, she made the trip back and forth to the arcades, buying material upon material—some for grenades, some for purposes written down only in Tally’s ledger. Nights found her at the small mirror in the washroom, practising Mimicry with the face-shaping model Tally had recorded in Gregory’s library. As she sat in the dim light, Tally overlaid a pale-blue map of the muscles onto her reflection in the mirror. Then Maureen would tilt her head, or clench her teeth, or simply hold her breath, and her jaw would shift, just slightly, so that for a heartbeat a stranger gazed back at her from the glass.

*The left cheek is lagging,* Tally told her.

*The left cheek is tired,* Maureen replied silently.

*A tired cheek will get you killed.*

In the corridors, a girl sat on her doorstep, sewing magic stones into her hem with shaking hands. A boy leaned into a drain, retching quietly. No one spared Maureen a second glance as she passed by with her bulging sacks. She was just another drop in a river of fear.

On the final night, Maureen counted the drawer of grenades once more, feeling each cold shell with her fingers. Ninety-five. Her hands didn’t tremble. That had to be a good sign.

Then an old, dry, unhurried voice filled every corridor, every room, all at once.

“All wizard apprentices will assemble. You depart for the secret realm. The trial begins.”

It was Augurit, Steward of the Dark Forest.

*You ready?* Tally asked.

*As ready as I’ll ever be,* she responded silently.

*Then so am I.*
