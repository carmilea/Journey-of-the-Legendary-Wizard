The smoke was ripped away by the wind as it blew across the clearing, leaving only a few grey rags to flutter in the breeze. Maureen’s ears still rang from the explosion, but her eyes were clear enough to take in the sight of destruction.

Where the shell had landed the earth was a bowl of blackened ground. The impact had shattered and inverted it, leaving a crater that still steamed. The ring of pine needles surrounding the lip of the crater burned low, filling the air with their sharp scent. Shrapnel had torn white scars into the bark of trees that stood near the blast.

Maureen walked to the edge of the crater. More than ten days spent locked up in the laboratory had gone into this. She’d overlaid the alchemy of the secret realm upon a memory of something from Jing Sheng’s world. A hand grenade. A black, egg-shaped shell no larger than a man’s palm, circled by silver runes. A simple weapon, but one she had no problem calling what it was.

Alchemical grenade.

For a heartbeat, as the orange bloom of the explosion rose from the ground, it seemed to Maureen like she was reliving the last moments of Jing Sheng’s life. A fireball bloomed from beneath the plane, swallowing it, and her, in flame.

Her fingers closed over the second shell in her pouch, but they did not twitch with the desire to throw.

*Verdict?* she asked silently.

*No cold voice out here to announce degrees,* Tally said, a hint of wistfulness in its tone. *So I calculated it myself. Crater width compared against Room 21 log.* [Alchemical grenade. Single use. Blast + fire, splash. Est. 10°.]

Ten. Her pulse quickened at that, and she allowed it to. Ten degrees was where Official Wizard spells began. That was the effective ceiling for anything an apprentice could cast. A dozen of these things thrown together could kill powerful apprentices outright and leave even a full Wizard bleeding.

*You built that ceiling out of a memory,* Tally noted.

*A weapon,* Maureen answered silently.

*A memory of a weapon. And Jing Sheng would have hated it.*

*Jing Sheng isn’t here.*

A crooked grin slipped loose before Maureen could suppress it. *Can’t beat me? Fine. I keep throwing until you stop getting up.*

*Logging: Keep throwing until opponent stops getting up,* Tally said. *That is a strategy.*

*It is one,* Maureen agreed.

*That is a sentence,* Tally told her. *A dozen shells is a strategy.*

Tally totalled up the cost of a dozen.

*Hillan metal makes up almost all the cost,* it noted. [Hillan per shell: ~7 magic stones.] *Two of your Recovery vials at Gandolf’s old price, gone in a single throw.*

Maureen finished the math herself, faster than she liked.

Thirty shells. Two hundred stones. Months at the bench, breathing in burnt hair every time a batch failed.

The shell in her pouch suddenly felt twice as heavy as it had when it left the lab. The grin slid off her face.

So, she put the pleasure away where it belonged. These were tools. They would help her, but they were not the source of her strength. Her own strength was what counted. She wasn’t going to start leaning on an iron egg.

[Recall: Day 26] sat in the corner of her sight. Gregory’s residence lay near the Dark Forest, and until today Maureen would have made the trip back with nothing but her own strength and a Seal whose glow was almost gone. Now she had teeth. Time to leave Wintercold: buy as much Hillan as she could afford, and settle into her new house.

At the treeline, her people were only now starting to unstick from their places. Hank’s hand still lay flat against the carriage wheel, his meticulously combed hair now blown out of place by the wind. Jess had his sword halfway out of its scabbard. Bram stood with his feet planted like a fence post that had taken root. Marla’s knuckles were white as she gripped her own hilt.

Four pulses still jumped at four throats. *Jess has not yet sheathed his blade,* Tally noted.

*They’ve never seen a wizard’s weapon go off in daylight,* Maureen answered silently.

Tally said, *They have now. It will take them a long while to forget.*

Maureen looked them over, one by one. Old Hank could keep the ledgers for any lord. Jess was a captain that a marquis would be proud to call his own. Marla’s hands could hold a blade for anyone. Bram’s feet had already dug in, become part of this land. None of them could follow her where she was going.

Hank was the first to meet her look, his silver-white hair shining in the sunlight. The middle finger of his right hand began to tap against his thigh, the way it tapped down a column whose total he didn’t like. A bound man knew what he was worth when he was no longer useful. He looked away from Maureen and toward the horse.

* * *

The carriage wheels crunched over frozen gravel as they entered Wintercold City. The streets were still, with coal smoke hanging low between the buildings. Frost furred the gutters. On the carriage box, Old Hank sat straight as an arrow, his whip moving almost imperceptibly in his right hand.

Dickens stood on the front steps, his boots muddy up to the ankles. His eyes went once to the east wing, where the side corridor led off from the main banquet hall, where Lauren had lain on the red rug.

“The Marquis is resting, milady,” Dickens said.

“Then wake him.”

Dickens bowed lower than Maureen had ever seen him bow to his own master, then left.

The flower hall was warm and damp compared to the streets outside. The panes were frosted grey, and forced lilies and dark red winter roses were pressed against them everywhere. A serving maid placed the tea before them—horse milk boiled to a thick cream, with honey. Already a skin was forming on the top of the pale yellow liquid.

Marquis Umas entered the room, beads of sweat dotting his forehead despite the quality of his wool clothing. His hair was combed flat, but he smoothed his beard twice before he even took his seat. This was a man dragged away from something urgent and important.

*Sweat at his collar,* Tally reported to Maureen. *Fresh mud on his boots, too. The beard twice. Trouble on your border.*

*Dinan,* Maureen answered silently. Tally told her that it had nothing on Dinan except his name.

“Umas,” Maureen said, “I’m leaving.”

The Marquis’s cup rattled in its saucer, sloshing tea onto the table as he half rose from his seat. “Leaving, my lady? Leaving the castle, or going on a journey, or—”

“Wintercold. I came to keep Adrian’s promise, and it’s kept.” She tilted her head to take a sip of her tea. Thick and fatty, it coated her tongue with sweetness.

He settled back into his chair, though the Marquis was not a man who ever truly settled.

“Yes. You paid me a viscountcy for one rebel. How very . . . generous of you. I’d have taken less.”

His face flushed darker. “You took Feren’s whole—”

“I know what I took.”

Maureen Olsen sat, hands folded serenely before her, maintaining her gracious smile. Across the table, Umas was sweating. Her stillness was doing all the work for her. The milk was rich and creamy and thick on her tongue, and far too sweet.

“My lady,” Umas finally said, licking his lips. “Half the province is saying there’s a witch up on my back mountain. Dinan is shouting loudest, and he hasn’t sent a man over the river since spring. That’s your doing.” His fingers went to his collar.

*No fidget, voice level, perfect guest’s face. Collar. Dinan?*

Tally scored her performance silently. Maureen did not ask about the other marquis’s territory. It was not her war.

*Umas wants his scarecrow to keep standing in the field.*

Tally added Umas’s telltale sign to its calculations.

*Then let him keep the coat.*

“My people,” Maureen said aloud. “They’re not like you or me. They can’t follow me into the Mystics’ world.” She set the cup down, leaving half of the tea undrunk. “I want them placed.”

“Hank,” Maureen continued, “was a court treasurer. He runs my fief more efficiently than Dickens runs this house.”

Umas’s eyes flicked to the door. “One of yours watching over my accounts?”

“He’d keep them honest. I’m sure your clerks would not appreciate the effort.”

Dickens stood at the door, ears flushing red, eyes fixed on a distant point upon the wall. The mud that had been on his boots had dried in the warm room, leaving twin crescents of dark earth upon the tile floor.

“And Jess, Marla, Bram . . .” Maureen said. “You’ve seen my roads.”

Umas had, and his tax assessors had returned with nothing to report.

“We’ll find them good positions,” Umas said, leaning forward. “But the title is still yours, and so is the fief.”

“Even if I don’t come back?”

“Especially then. While your name is upon it, no one will dare desire it.”

*He’s buying your name, Maureen. Buying its weight, the same way the Duke bought you through Richard.*

*Let him buy. It costs me nothing.*

Tally opened up an Umas column anyway, placing it beside Skran’s.

Maureen smiled pleasantly, giving Umas the assurances he required. The tea sat, undisturbed, its skin growing thick and waxlike upon the surface. Beyond the frost-grey windowpanes, a crow shook loose a cluster of icicles from the eaves.

The marquis himself escorted her out to the carriage. He didn’t even bother with a hat, despite the cold. “If you ever do come back, my lady . . .”

“I’ll make certain you hear of it before I arrive.”

Umas stood for a moment, unable to tell if that was a promise or not.

*Neither am I, quite,* she thought.

As Maureen climbed into the carriage, Tally marked a long breath behind her—Umas’s. Not by smell, but by sound alone.

*He’d been holding his breath since you said you were leaving.*

* * *

“Albert’s, Hank.”

“Yes, my lady.”

The whip cracked once, and the carriage swung into another street.

Albert’s parlour was overheated, and it stank of the liniment he rubbed on his chest to keep his breathing clear. A cloud of lilac hung in the air as well, in an attempt to mask the liniment’s smell. The coal fire in the grate hissed as the flames dried the water from the coal. Thick velvet curtains kept out the light and the cold, and the sofa was overstuffed, broken down in the middle where Albert had sat for so many years.

“M-Maureen! My friend, what an honour!” Albert laughed too loudly, and his round face squeezed his eyes nearly shut. They performed the traditional wizards’ greeting, and Albert bowed a fraction more deeply than Maureen did.

Tally noted that Albert had pulled out the lilac-scented handkerchief to mop his brow twice before Maureen had even seated herself.

*He always sweats,* she answered silently.

*Not this fast,* Tally replied.

“I’m leaving Wintercold,” Maureen said as soon as she was seated.

Albert tried to stall, mouth half open, waiting to see if she would elaborate. When she didn’t, he studied her expression. She meant it. He waved his steward out of the room to collect the others.

While they waited, Maureen questioned him about Hillan. Albert wheezed and shook his jowls as he spoke. There wasn’t a lot of the ore in Wintercold, but he knelt before the iron box that stood beneath his writing desk, unlocked it himself, and pulled out a small lot that he had taken as surety against a bad loan made to a Grey Dove trader.

The ingots came out wrapped in oilcloth, and despite the protective wrapping, they were still cold enough to make Maureen’s fingers ache through her gloves. The metal glinted where light caught on its edges, crawling along like the sun’s reflection on frost. The ingots were heavier than they looked.

Albert named a price, and they did not haggle.

[Magic stones: −80. Hillan: small lot, est. 12 shells.]

*There will be more lots like this one available in wizard settlements along the road,* Tally said. *We should start a list.*

*Of settlements?*

*Of shops.*

*You’ll want both.*

The other apprentices arrived, shaking the cold from their cloaks as they entered. Daphne already had her sewing in her lap, the needle a blur as she worked and muttered all at once. Aubrey placed his cane upright on the rug, and it remained standing without support. Blake added a fourth dimension to the scent of the room with a cloak drenched in rose and ambergris. Before his outer coat even came off, the smell of roses was strong enough to make Maureen’s nose twitch.

“Leaving?” Daphne asked, her needle never missing a beat. “Whatever for? Wintercold’s lovely, nobody bothers anybody, and if it’s materials, Grey Dove’s only a few days—”

Aubrey tugged sharply on her sleeve, giving her a hard look. She closed her mouth and went back to talking to herself, though now she kept it to a mutter and her needle flew even faster along the seam.

Maureen caught Aubrey’s eye, and he looked away. That look. He’d been watching when Sawin’s runners had turned to grey bone dust on the road. Everyone here was handling her like a cup full to the brim. Careful, careful.

Aubrey rose from his chair, moving stiffly, like a lectern on legs. “You have your own road ahead of you, Maureen. May it take you far upon the wizard’s path.” He paused, and then added quietly, “Good luck.”

His voice never moved half a tone, his hands never trembled as they rested on the head of his cane.

*No tells observed.*

*Not proof.*

*File the wish anyway.*

Blake opened his mouth as Albert mentioned the Grey Dove trader, then he closed it. Daphne said something about Grey Dove, and Blake opened his mouth again and shut it. Albert mentioned that Grey Dove hadn’t had any Hillan in bulk either, and for a third time Blake’s lips parted.

Nothing came out.

*Three aborted openings. All at “Grey Dove.” His eyes keep darting to your hands, to your pouch. Never to your face.*

*He’s grieving.*

*No. He’s counting. Like me. Is that in dispute?*

*Logged.*

Maureen’s fingers settled onto the flap of her pouch, where they rested. Blake sank down into the broken middle of the sofa, his face going an ashy grey beneath his trimmed beard. The rose and ambergris hung about him like a cloud of smoke over a recently snuffed lamp.

At the door, Daphne bit off her thread and said without looking up, “Wear something on your head. The north road’s wicked,” before returning to her mutterings.

Albert’s brittle smile held right up until the door swung shut behind Maureen.

As the carriage pulled away from the curb, Maureen caught sight of Blake standing at the parlour window. Watching the carriage. What it carried. Not her.

*Remember the face.*

*Already done.*

“Home,” she told Old Hank, sitting up on the driver’s bench.

* * *

Night had come down hard over Maureen’s castle. The tallow candles in the great hall leaned and shivered in the draughts that crept in around the edges of the doors and windows. The cold came up through the soles of her boots from the flagstones. Out on the walls of the keep, the guards called the watch to each other.

Old Hank, Jess, Marla and Bram came into the hall. As always when they came before her in the high chair, the three knights went down on one knee while Old Hank stood a step behind them, his hands clasped before him.

“I’m leaving.”

Jess’s head came up first. “Where, my lady? Did we fail you?”

“My lady, you—” Old Hank began, then stopped. They all saw her face. This wasn’t a ride to Grey Dove. This was for good.

“No. You kept my roads. That’s why Umas wants you. I can’t take you with me. Where I’m going, you wouldn’t last a week.” Umas had promised them good posts, she said. If there was anything else within reason they desired, it was theirs for the asking.

Marla’s palms pressed flat against her knees. Jess’s jaw worked once. Only Bram was completely still. The candlelight burnished Jess’s red hair to copper. No eyes rose higher than Maureen’s boots.

*All four had looked at one another before looking at her,* Tally reported. *Old Hank’s lips had moved before any words had come out, just as they had the night of the old rules.*

*I saw,* she told Tally silently.

*They’ve had no other roof over their heads since their names were cut out,* Maureen added.

*That fits,* Tally responded.

“I leave the day after tomorrow. Think about it. Now go and sleep.”

The knights rose as one, the scraping of steel on stone loud in the hall. All four bowed, then backed out of the hall.

In the morning, Old Hank came to Maureen as she sat down to breakfast.

“My lady, I’d like to stay in Wintercold. Grow old here. I’m too old to keep up this pace.”

Frost had feathered the window. A bowl of porridge sat to one side of her plate, a heel of black bread to the other. Old Hank rested his right hand on the edge of the table. The ink stain on the pad of his middle finger stood out darker than the skin around it, years of Maureen’s ledgers worked into it.

“You are old, Hank,” she said with a smile. “A thousand gold. That should see you to the end.”

[Fief strongbox: gold -1,000. Hank's own entry.]

 *Paying the man who keeps the receipts from his own receipts,* Tally said. It was very pleased with itself.

*He’ll appreciate that,* Maureen answered silently.

*You like anyone who keeps receipts.*

*So do I,* she admitted.

“Thank you, my lady.” Old Hank’s voice caught on the words. He bowed low, and did not rise again.

She looked at the ink-stained finger, and did not speak for a breath. In the months he had been on the fief, Old Hank had maintained the books to the copper, though no one would ever have checked. Her spoon rested quietly on the rim of the bowl.

Her decision came quickly, once she let herself make it. She had four bound slaves. They were of no use to her beyond the gate. Allowing them to choose was free; it made her seem generous. Kindness so cheaply purchased was the least expensive item in the castle, and she knew the price of everything there.

So the binding stayed. Julo’s house array, completed with her own mana in the slave market, remained intact in all four. She hadn’t even touched it.

*The bindings,* Tally asked once. *All four still active. Intentional?*

*Yes,* Maureen answered silently.

*Logged,* Tally said.

*If you had hesitated,* Tally added a beat later, *I would have asked again.*

Maureen didn’t hesitate.

Jess and Marla exchanged a glance. “We’ll serve the Marquis, my lady,” Jess said. Both knights brought their fist down on their breastplates, and the steel rang through the room.

Marla’s knuckles remained pressed against the metal. Scarred, thickened by calluses, they were for half a breath Aunt Mary’s hands, Maureen’s neighbour back in the Skran slums during her own childhood, wringing washing until the knuckles cracked red from the lye. Then they became the hands of a knight again, the calluses and scars rough against the polished steel.

Bram spoke last, and slowly. He wanted to stay on the fief, become the captain of the guard under whomever Umas sent to run the place. “Someone should keep the roads,” he said.

She agreed at once. *One of yours stays with the land, then,* Tally noted.

*Bound,* Maureen answered silently. *Steady.*

Tally filed Bram under the fief. *If you ever come back,* it said, *I’ll know whose face to look for at the gate.*

Over the next day and a half, she erased herself. The bodies of the dead from the back mountain still lay in the cellar cells. She dissolved them, and sluiced the stones clean. She shattered the great glass tube that had held the Scarlet Potion brewing. She unpicked all the concealments she had laid, and scrubbed the laboratory down to the bare stone.

Tally accompanied her room by room, checking its record of the castle against what Maureen was leaving behind. It matched what she still saw against its records, and pointed out the discrepancies. A scorch mark on the floor of the study, a faint trace of residue in the drain of one cell. She dealt with each. *There is nothing left on file that any wizard can discover,* Tally told her.

Lastly she stood in the empty laboratory. The lye had done its work, and there were no other smells to taint the air. For a moment, it was not her castle, but Jing Sheng’s bench at the end of a long day, wiped clean, lamps blown out, ready for tomorrow. Except that she would never return to this place tomorrow. She closed the door.

The wagon Hank had bought her was wide enough to hold everything she meant to keep. The grenades lay in their padded packs, Albert’s Hillan nestled among them, and the potions stood in a wooden box. She had wrapped up the bronze book and Old Farry’s book, and they lay next to her black cloth-wrapped Sodara trunk, hauled out from its place in the corner of her bedroom. The high chair remained, as did the title and the four people who came with it. On the second morning, the light was thin and pale, and the wood of the wagon wheels gleamed silver from frost. Her breath came before her in great white clouds. The yard was full of people. The patrol guards stood in rows, the servants stood behind them. The horse stood before her, stamping and blowing white clouds through its bit.

Old Hank came forward with a ledger, tied up with a length of string. “The last accounts for the fief, my lady. Written up last night.” He handed it over, and she felt its weight in her hand, then put it on the seat beside her.

The entire yard bowed with him, Jess, Marla and Bram standing at the front. “May peace keep you, my lady.”

“May peace keep you,” echoed all the lines.

She smiled at them, then turned and climbed up onto the driver’s box. She took the reins into her own hands and set the horse in motion, rolling through the gateway and out from the keep. She didn’t look back.

The wagon’s wooden wheels jolted over the frozen ruts. The white world fell away behind her, the castle walls dwindling to nothing. Her hands held the reins steady.

Tally noted that she had wrapped the leather traces twice around her fist before letting them run free, and opened a new line. [Recall: Day 28]

Then, wrapping itself around her thoughts with a friendly warmth, it said, *Just us.*

*It has always been just us,* she answered silently.

*Yes, but now it’s quieter,* Tally said.
