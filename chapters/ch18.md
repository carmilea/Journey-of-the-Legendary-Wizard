Evans stayed in his carriage throughout the journey, leaving Emily to run the convoy. The Third-Class Wizard Apprentice had set them on a path that would take them through this forest to the Principality of Cansia. From there they would head two days south to Haileng Port and take a ship back to the Dark Forest.

The seeds travelled in silence, having little to say about the journey and no say in where they went. The only sounds were the creaking of harnesses, the soft splat of wheels turning in the wet leaf mould underfoot, and the sound of the black knights' boots hitting the ground in a single rhythm.

Maureen continued her training in secret behind her carriage curtains. Tally kept note of each session, logging the time that Maureen managed to hold the spell active.

[Activation: 6s … 7s … 9s]

Ten seconds please, Maureen thought silently.

Nine and a half seconds is all your body is willing to provide at the moment.

Each time the Seal's energy drain left the same dull ache behind Maureen's ribs, as though she'd spent a day hauling water up from the well. She was slowly becoming used to it.

Between sessions of activating the Blood Seal of Thorns, Maureen meditated on the Spirit Rune. Tally counted out the seconds of the Silver Light rhythm for her and laid pale blue arcs over her sword drills. Maureen focused intently on the exercises, correcting each error the first time it was shown to her. Very good, that's the way. You didn't need any repeats this time.

The fights between the old black robe seeds and the new seeds had become worse. Rumour had it that one of the new seeds had been killed, but no one had reported it and Emily didn't seem to care.

Anthony had visited her carriage twice since they began the trip, both times inviting her to join his group. Both times Maureen refused and the young man's face had soured with annoyance. The second time he had even walked out before she finished answering him.

Noted. He did not remain to hear your answer. I think he is resentful of you.

Fine by me, Tally.

After that, Anthony didn't visit her again. As the feud between the new and old black robe seeds grew, so did Anthony's standing in the group. Before long he was the figurehead of the new seeds. Maureen didn't pay any attention to it. As long as she was left alone, she wouldn't demonstrate her strength or punish anyone. At night she strung the bell cord across her carriage door and slept with the sleeve crossbow armed, listening to the dripping of the forest overhead.

* * *

Maureen finished her sword practice, mopping the sweat off her neck with a towel. The breeze was cool against her damp skin. Ahead of her smoke from the camp drifted through the trees. She crossed the clearing and found Aaron sitting on the ground at its edge, looking into the trees. His face was bruised and swollen, and his eyes were filled with hatred. Maureen crouched down beside him.

“What happened?”

Aaron started at her voice. “Nothing.”

He put his hand up to his face to hide the damage. Not that it was necessary. Tally told her everything anyway.

Fresh purple bruising over older yellow-green bruising approximately three days old.

How many times?

More than one. No additional data available.

“Are you -” Aaron started, then stopped as a loud voice shouted from the trees.

“Well well well. Look who’s here after all!”

A ring of tall thin black robe wearing youths closed around Maureen and Aaron, all of them grinning. Aaron scrambled back.

> Five.

Leader plus four.

Left one, knife at belt.

They see only a girl. An advantage.

Keep counting.

Maureen turned to face the blond youth who had shouted.

“Who are you lot?” she asked.

The blond youth looked surprised for a moment, before he laughed. “Are you going to meddle, girl?” He sneered.

Maureen ignored him, turning back to Aaron. “What do you want with him?”

The blond youth stepped forward, and the others moved with him. “Trash like him should be emptying chamber pots and scrubbing latrines.” The gang laughed, and Aaron clenched his fists, dropping his head so his hair hid his face. His skin flushed red, then white.

Maureen patted Aaron’s shoulder gently, then turned back to face the gang. “You lot. Get lost.”

The blond youth gave a nasty smile. “A girl has some nerve, hasn’t she? Asking to die too. Well, let’s teach her a lesson.” The gang moved quickly, rushing Maureen.

> Knife. Wrist.

Maureen caught the knife hand and snapped the wrist back. The boy screamed and fell, clutching his broken wrist.

> Knee, left.

Maureen’s foot lashed out, catching the next boy’s knee and snapping it sideways.

It took about thirty heartbeats for all five boys to end up on the ground, their bones cracking and breaking, their screams echoing around the trees as the camp smoke drifted between them. When the last boy was down, the blond youth was on his back, staring up at Maureen.

“Wait,” the blond youth gasped. “I’m a follower of Rheas. I’m...” he gulped, “a follower of Rheas!”

“Do you want to continue?” Maureen asked softly.

Tally said nothing.

The blond youth shivered on the ground, looking up into Maureen’s cold black eyes. Tally logged the flinch.

“Rheas won’t let you off! He’ll come and teach you a proper lesson!” The blond youth scrambled to his feet and limped off with his men, all of them holding their arms, wincing with pain.

Tally opened a file in front of Maureen’s eyes.

> Rheas: high aptitude. Has followers.
> Now hostile.

I’ll keep out of his way, she thought at it.

That is no longer up to you. Tally’s voice was quiet.

Aloud, Maureen turned to Aaron. “Are you all right?”

Aaron shook his head. He breathed deeply several times. “Thank you,” he said. “Thank you.”

Maureen began to ask why they had picked on him, but Aaron shouted, “Don’t ask why!” then thanked her again before staggering off, still panting from pain.

She stood there for a moment, feeling the breeze cooling the sweat under her hood, frowning as she watched his back disappear through the trees.

* * *

“Maureen,” Richard said, leaning against a pine tree, letting the sticky bark cover his palms with resin. His face was unusually serious as he spoke, and the smoke from the cooking fires hung low between the carriages, not dissipating in the still evening air.

“The rules here are harsh. There is a strict hierarchy, and the people at the bottom—low-aptitude seeds—stay at the bottom forever, whether they’re grade three or grade two or even grade one.”

He looked down at his feet, and then continued. “At best, they get to become Third-Class Wizard Apprentices. Some of them don’t even get that far.”

Maureen nodded. Now she understood why Aaron had been the target of their bullying. She was about to ask Tally if this was accurate, but Tally was already doing it for her, comparing what Richard was saying against its measurements at Sunset Canyon.

> Correct. Grade three aptitude puts Aaron at the bottom rung of the ladder.

“What about Carey?” Maureen asked.

“Oh, he’s all right,” Richard said. “Everyone treated him well, even the old black-robes left him alone. High-aptitude seeds are pretty rare. Just Carey and Rheas.”

Carey . . . working in the Skran gardens, bits of soil under his fingernails when he came to do the test. Aaron, calling a knight a fool and getting away with it. Not now, please, Maureen told the chip silently.

I’m simply being accurate, it replied.

Richard’s face brightened, and he changed the subject. “Anyway, enough of this gloomy stuff. After all the trouble lately, Lucy has been a little frightened. She let herself lean against me and even put her arms around me for a few moments. I was thrilled.”

Maureen smiled—with her mouth, not her eyes—and let Richard continue talking until the fire burned low.

* * *

The journey through the forest lasted about a month, after which the convoy rumbled onto the wet stone quays of Haileng Port. The sea wind carried a faint smell of fish, and Tally dutifully added an entry to its olfactory data. [Sea smell: fish, brine, tar.] It sounded pleased with itself about the new category. Leave room for the ship, Tally, Maureen thought. The seeds stood with their mouths open at the sight of a ship bobbing in the green water, a vessel so massive that its masts and spars blotted out the sky. In front of the ship lay a giant tortoise, a beast the size of a small mountain. Its gigantic head was raised to look at Evans, who floated in midair a short distance from the animal. The tortoise’s eyes were respectful and obedient, like a dog’s. Crusts of barnacles and weed covered its shell, and its slow breathing made clouds of condensation rise from the cold water that lapped at its sides.

[Scale: exceeds reference objects. Scale: exceeds reference objects. . . .] Tally gave up halfway through comparing the size of the beast to the height of the ship’s mast. [Spike: known source.]

“A bloodline creature bred from an Aoling Tortoise and siren blood,” Evans murmured to himself. “Truly the best choice for a long sea voyage.”

Evans dissolved into a surge of dark energy particles and reappeared on the deck of the ship. [No data.] Tally sounded tired of saying that.

“We can come up now,” Evans said quietly, but each seed heard him as if he stood right next to them. [no data] on how the wizard’s voice carried so clearly over the screaming gulls, the waves slapping against the wooden pilings of the harbour, and the general bustle of the port. Maureen followed Richard up the ladder to the ship, and one after another the rest of the seeds climbed aboard.

From some high point on the deck, Emily spoke. “I expect you all to behave and follow the rules.” Then she turned her back on them without any expression on her face. The seeds went silently to their assigned rooms. No one dared step out of line yet, not with Dark Forest wizards watching them.

Belowdecks, the timber smelled wet from the sea air. The floor rose and fell slowly with the breathing of the mountain-sized beast.

* * *

Maureen stood alone by the rail in the dark. A cold wind blew off the water, and the hull creaked as the ship moved. Far below the beast breathed slowly, each exhalation rocking it.

Ahead was the night sea, but astern the continent had thinned to a grey line on the horizon. She could feel it going even though she couldn’t put a name to what she was leaving behind.

Tally’s voice sounded in her head, warm and comforting.

Do you want me to count the lights on the shore for you? As they go out I can tell you how many there are.

Yes Tally, count them.

Nine…eight…seven…the numbers sounded softly inside her head, one number for every light that disappeared.

From along the deck an oil lamp swung closer, its light playing over the deck until it found her. She stood alone in the night, her arms resting on the wooden rail. Richard peered at her through the gloom.

“Maureen, what’s wrong?”

Tally fell silent, so she could answer.

She looked up at Richard, paused for a moment, then shook her head. “Nothing.”
