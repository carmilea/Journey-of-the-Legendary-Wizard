The inside of the carriage was completely dark—blacker than the inside of an eyelid. It was so black that even when she held her hand up to her face, she could not see its shape. There was nothing but the feeling of the dark particles crawling across the walls like cold ants. Outside, the altered beasts roared, a thunderous sound bouncing around inside the wheelless carriage as though it were a pipe made of stone.

Since there was no other gauge, Tally counted Maureen’s pulse, then told her that the ride had taken just over an hour—perhaps longer. It sounded more impressive put that way, Tally said, especially in the dark again.

*I know,* Maureen thought back. *You told me that last time.*

*That’s because it’s true,* Tally said smugly.

The carriage slowed down, rocked, and came to a stop. The door swung open, revealing a grey daylight, and Maureen climbed down with the few other Dark Forest apprentices who had survived. They stood on the wet black flagstones before the gate.

The same lid of flat grey cloud hung in the sky as ever—it pressed down upon people for months at a time until they snapped at one another. But today the young men and women breathed in the air as though it were the smell of freshly baked bread, and Maureen felt the tension leave her own shoulders. That unsettled her more than the secret realm trial had: A place this cruel had begun to feel like home.

Hugh was back at the gate, its trident planted firmly beside it. It began its old routine without preamble. “Ho ho! Little apprentices, b-back again? Tell the great Lord Hugh the p-password, or I’ll swallow you wh-whole—”

Then Iman tugged hard on its stone arm, nearly yanking it off its feet.

Tally displayed a readout in the corner of Maureen’s vision. [Hugh. Iman. Constructs: absent.]

*Hugh’s home,* Tally said.

*Why?*

*No data available,* Tally said. *Someone stopped walking the fence.*

“S-S-Steward... L-Lord Steward...” Hugh spun around, scowling deeply at the interruption of its routine. It followed Iman’s rigid stare down to the old man standing at the head of the apprentices. The lizard-headed construct looked as though it might leap right off its plinth. Its tongue lolled out of its mouth, a long, thick, scarlet muscle that hung almost to the ground. Drool dripped from the tip of the tongue and hissed as it hit the flagstones. Five metres of living stone stood up straight and tall, like a kitchen boy caught with his fingers in the jam pot.

Augurit smiled up at the two statues, and the deep wrinkles that lined his face softened. “Hugh. Iman. You both look well lately.” It was the voice of a grandfather standing at his garden gate, not a voice anyone ever used to address constructs.

Iman, all fangs and solid stone, nodded up and down, as if agreeing to something. Then, suddenly, it pulled its mouth into what might have passed for meekness on a creature less frightening. “Thank you, Steward. Well. We’re well,” it said.

Beside it, Hugh could only grin like an idiot. “Heh. Heh heh.”

Those were the first spoken words recorded for Iman in the last two years, Tally noted. [Augurit Jones. HIGH energy radiation source. Finer: no data.]

*Kind to the statues,* Maureen thought. *You don’t like that, do you?*

*No. I do not like kindness in someone so powerful.*

*But you like Adrian.*

*He earned it.*

She watched the exchange between Augurit and the statues the same way she read numbers, the way Tally did. It wasn’t just what Augurit said, but how he said it. The statues were afraid of his mildness. This was a man whose voice had never needed to rise to be obeyed. Whatever he asked for had always come true.

Augurit gave the statues a last nod before turning to lead the apprentices through the gate and across the courtyard. A few wizards stood on the terrace, watching. They turned around to stare as Augurit brought his apprentices back from their trials. Maureen caught glances of open surprise, but nothing more than that.

The smell of incense hung over the courtyard, as always. But underneath it lay the stench of rot. She counted the number of black robes that walked with her. The count was short. Each flat black flagstone in the courtyard was beaded with damp. The watching wizards’ eyes slid over each apprentice’s face, as if they were pricing fruit at market.

[Probes: 3+, by particle sense.] Faint threads of particles brushed across the crowd, light as cobweb on the skin. *Keep your face,* it told her silently.

A breath later, Tally added, sounding pleased, *You’re doing it already.*

Augurit did not raise his voice. He never needed to. “That’s enough looking,” he said. “I’ll send you the details by message shortly.”

The faint probing threads vanished immediately. The watching wizards bowed their heads to Augurit and walked away. The apprentices bowed in turn, then scattered to their rooms to rest, just as Augurit had told them.

Tally filed the sentence away, next to the oath Augurit had invoked at the secret realm’s gate.

*Two times now,* it said, *one quiet line from this old man has stopped powerful people mid-stride.*

*I know,* she answered silently. *Keep that file close.*

* * *

The rooms were exactly as she had left them. Every brass fitting was in its place and the lights woke as she walked in. She had no injuries to treat, just a tiredness she didn’t understand sitting behind her eyes like damp sand.

She ran a bath as hot as it would go, and scrubbed the realm out of her skin. Gravel and dried blood, the sticky film the sweet air had left. Then she lay down on the bed, and sleep took her whole. Between one breath and the next, she was gone.

In the mirror of the washroom, just before she went under, she had seen short black hair cropped close to the skull, and Jing Sheng’s face beneath. Jack was gone.

Maureen woke slowly. She pressed the heel of her hand to her forehead.

*Tally? How long was I out?*

[Sleep: 17h 02m. Deep.]

*I see. Her body thought it was owed some time,* Tally said.

*I’m a Third-Class Wizard Apprentice,* she replied silently. *My body does what I tell it.*

*Your body walked through the realm,* Tally said, *and I think it’s allowed an opinion on the matter.*

Her limbs were heavy and warm, like blocks of stone left out in the sun. Maureen didn’t like unfamiliar sensations that she couldn’t explain, so she treated them like data instead.

She flexed each finger in turn, watching them obey her commands.

[Spirit 11.2. Mana 11.2. Full.]

Tally displayed her status line for her. *I am guessing - and this is by inference only - that the two Skoll fruits are still settling into your system. That may be part of why you slept so long.*

Maureen laughed softly at that, reaching up to touch her nose.

Her eyes fell to the black cloth bag from the muster, now weighted with the loot she’d picked up carefully while exploring the realm. There were materials of all kinds inside, rare components drawn from the collections of apprentices of every order. Resources brought up from the Underworld by the dark elves themselves. Even an Official Wizard would want these things.

“These can’t be seen,” she murmured, staring into the bag’s depths.

They had to be hidden properly.

She crouched down beside the bed, reaching out to tap on the third stone block away from its frame. “Manas.” The block of stone rippled like water, not making a sound as it pulled away to reveal a dark hollow within. The hollow went down far further than the floor outside should have allowed.

Maureen had cut this hollow during her preparation nights before the trial. It was an upgraded version of her old wall-cache illusion, the one from the speculative library book that Tally helped her simulate. The study’s cavity had been too small, so she’d scaled up the effect when she could. The hollow smelled slightly of resin from the Sodara trunk which already lay within, wrapped in the same black cloth that protected the rest of her treasure.

Tally kept a running inventory as Maureen worked to store her items.

[Black bag: materials, sorted.]

[Grenades: 34.]

[Sodara trunk: present.]

A new [Hollow] line appeared next to the old cache line, which still read 0.

The bag went in first, then Maureen unbuckled her grenade bandolier and the padded pack that held more of her resources. The bandolier and pack joined the trunk in the hollow. The Blood Seal of Thorns, the Silver Soul Token, the stone cards, and the bronze book stayed secure against her body. Freya’s sword remained sheathed at her hip.

*Rule’s the rule,* Tally noted.

Maureen agreed silently. Anything that could be found on her body was something she could defend with her own hands.

“Manas,” she whispered again.

The hollow obediently drew itself closed, the stone rippling back into place until it was just stone again. Maureen nodded once, then allowed herself a small, satisfied smile before she pushed herself back to her feet.

There was a knock at the door just as she stood up straight.

Her eyebrows drew together as she regarded the door. “Who is it?”

* * *

“It’s me!” came a voice from the other side. The door opened without waiting for an answer, and a young woman stepped through. Her eyes were already wet and she crossed the threshold in two quick steps, throwing her arms around Maureen. “Maureen! I’m so glad you’re back, I’m so glad you’re all right! I thought you were dead . . .”

Crushed herbs and lye soap wafted into Maureen’s nostrils, the soft white gauze of Winnie’s dress brushed against her cheek, and there was a definite crack in Winnie’s voice. Maureen stood stiff as a post, her arms hanging uselessly at her sides. She’d been completely taken off guard.

Maureen’s hands slowly rose, as if they’d only just received the message that they should return Winnie’s embrace. They moved awkwardly at first, patting Winnie’s back in uncertain thumps. “It’s all right, Winnie. I’m fine. Really, I’m fine.” It had taken over two years, but Winnie had apparently decided to start treating Maureen like a little sister. The girl felt a warmth spread beneath her ribs, a reaction she hadn’t planned for and couldn’t readily file away anywhere.

*Winnie didn’t hug you the last time, but she’s hugging you now. That’s significant,* Tally noted.

*Yes, yes it is,* Maureen agreed silently. *Log it as a hug.*

*I’ve logged two hugs. The first one was owed, so I put it in the books.*

Winnie finally pulled back, wiping her eyes with her wrist and staring at Maureen with a fixed gaze. “Your hair! What did you do—”

*Say it got in the way. Everyone in the trial cut something.* *Tally advised quickly.*

“It got in the way,” Maureen said with a shrug. “Everyone in there cut something off.”

*That’s a pretty good cover story. It’s plausible, it’s boring, and it’s perfect.*

Winnie blinked her eyes clear and took a deep breath to steady herself. “Mentor wants to see you.” She gestured for Maureen to follow, and this time led the girl past the corridor to the lab and onward into Adrian’s private residence.

It wasn’t an impressive place—just a small villa set in the center of a plain courtyard. There were flowers in clumps across the courtyard as well, now unfolding their petals to the sun. A trellis arced between two corners of the villa and wisteria flowed down its length like a purple waterfall. The sweet smell of honey and dust drifted up from the flowers. Sunlight lay on the flagstones in soft patterns, but there was no sky above to throw it. The shadows were just a little too short.

*No source. Not anywhere in my sight.* Tally mused.

*I know.* Maureen thought back silently.

*Will log as sun, provisionally.*

Adrian was seated in a chair in the courtyard, a book resting open on his knee and a cup of tea at his elbow. A delicate curl of steam rose from the cup and danced into the air.

His grey hair and glasses made him look like any other old man sitting in any garden. He could have been a shopkeeper or a minor functionary. There was nothing to suggest that he was both an apothecary and a wizard.

“Mentor,” Maureen said with a bow.

Adrian looked up from his book. Deep wrinkles opened across his face as he smiled. “Ah. Olsen. You’ve come.”

Maureen stood quietly as Adrian’s eyes studied her. Hand: tremor, slight. Skin: greyer than before the trial. Breathing: shallower. Tally read him by sight.

*He’s old.* Maureen thought back.

*Older than when we passed through the gate.*

“You are not hurt?” Adrian asked.

“A few bruises. Nothing more.”

Adrian studied her for another long moment through the lenses of his glasses, but asked no questions about the realm. “Good. Then rest. The rest can wait.”

When he set his teacup back down on its saucer it rattled ever so faintly against the porcelain. Once. Maureen kept her expression as immobile as the conjured sunlight.

One of the wisteria flowers shed a petal, which floated down to land on the open page of Adrian’s book. He brushed it away with a single finger, but left his hand resting flat against the page for just a little longer than was strictly necessary. As if keeping the page in place took more effort than he cared to admit.

Maureen stayed for a while, letting Adrian talk about the flowers in his courtyard, and said little herself. When she made her leave-takings, Winnie walked her out.

* * *

The lamps that ran in a line along the corridor burned low. The light from them shivered and blurred against the black stone of the walls, as though some great beast breathed upon them. Winnie walked close by her side, and their footsteps were a soft sound in the vast hush. Maureen looked straight ahead of her.

“Winnie. Mentor doesn’t seem well lately, does he?”

Winnie’s step faltered at that, and she cast a sidelong glance at Maureen. “I... I don’t know, Maureen. Mentor’s been... lately he’s...” Her lips opened on a word, then closed without uttering it.

Finally, it came out in a great sigh. “He wants me to be an Official Wizard. More and more with each month. To get the resources for my advancement, he’s agreed to brew potions for other wizards.”

Her voice dropped to a whisper. “The kind that hurt him to make.”

There was guilt in that sigh, and it explained something Maureen had noticed as she left for the trial. Winnie, who’d hugged her the day her first potion had worked properly, had kept her arms at her sides when Maureen had left. It was as though she couldn’t risk touching the girl.

Maureen’s brows drew together by just a fraction. She said nothing.

[Adrian: brewing for others, at a cost to himself. For Winnie.] Tally added that line to the file.

*Put that in a column of its own,* Maureen instructed Tally silently, *right next to where he refused Barok that night.*

*Already there,* Tally replied, its mental voice quiet. *I put it there first.*

The blow landed somewhere Maureen didn’t want to think about right now. I’m not in the habit of selling off my own apprentices, he had said, his eyes on her. But he was selling himself instead. One cup at a time.

She kept her voice light, as if this were merely an afterthought. “And Senior Brother Mace?”

“Mace?” Winnie blinked. “I don’t know, I don’t see him very often these days. It’s been a while since I’ve seen him.”

Mace: sister, Anna. Deceased.

Tally brought up the file without being asked.

Maureen’s pulse jumped fourteen beats.

*He is not here,* Tally said quietly. *You are in a corridor with Winnie. Breathe like Silver Light.*

She did.

*Long breath, long breath, long breath. Short breath.*

Anna’s last word in the smoke had been his name. It sat behind Maureen’s teeth, like a stone she couldn’t quite swallow or spit out.

She nodded, and said no more. She didn’t tell Winnie about Anna.

“Maureen,” Winnie said suddenly, twisting her head around to look at her. “Do you remember Lilith?”

The name caught her mid-stride.

Blue-crystal eyes. A laugh, hidden behind one slender hand. A black hood pulled down low over her face, standing in her doorway late one night.

Back when the Olivias were hunting her, Lilith had arrived with the Walker Family’s offer of protection. One hundred and fifty years of service. Unconditional obedience. Eighty percent of all potions created would go straight to the family coffers. Slavery, Maureen had told Lilith. The girl had left in tears.

Winnie spoke quietly, as if afraid of being overheard. “She’s a Walker. Her talent for pharmacology wasn’t ever that great, honestly. Mentor only took her in as a favour to her family. She’s stopped studying now. She’s gone home to them.”

Winnie glanced up at Maureen. “Before she left, she kept wanting to come and see you. It’s a pity, Maureen. You weren’t in the Dark Forest then.”

Maureen felt her throat tighten. The potioneer’s notebook was still sitting on her shelf, its leather cover worn soft at the corners from long use, not from the expense of stones but from the work it had seen.

Tally spoke up without being asked. *Still keeping it in the same folder as that night.*

*I know,* Maureen replied silently.

Tally was quick to respond.

*I know you do,* Tally said, *but I wanted to tell you anyway.*

Maureen was silent for another moment before nodding. “I understand.”

Winnie remained motionless for a long time, just looking at her. There were so many things tangled up in the senior apprentice’s face, it seemed difficult to know where to begin. *Two columns,* Tally said. *I can’t name either one of them.*

*Neither can I.* Maureen thought back.

Winnie let out a soft breath and smiled sadly. “Rest properly, Maureen. You’ve only just come back from the secret realm trial, and there’s still a lot waiting for you to face.” With a word of goodbye, she turned and made her way towards her own quarters.

Maureen stood alone in the corridor, the low-burning lamps making the light shiver and blur along the black stone. She could hear the soft echo of Winnie’s footsteps, the scent of lye soap and herbs lingering in the air from the other apprentice’s white gauze dress.

Tally counted the footsteps for her, all the way until there were none left to count. Just as it had once done with the lights upon the shore.
