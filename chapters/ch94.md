Maureen walked back from the washroom, down a corridor of doors that were all the same. The brass locks had become dulled, a thin layer of dust covering first one, then another, and another. Step after step, her footfalls were the only sound against the unyielding black stone.

Tally had counted the number of doors that hadn’t opened since their return.

[Apprentices in residence: about 200.]

[Before the trial: over 400.]

Maureen told it to put that information on a shelf, and it did so. Less than half had come home.

Winnie was in Maureen’s study, perched on the edge of the chair. “Uh...” she began, searching for words. “You kind of guessed what happened, right? Not everyone went in. The core members of the wizard families were exempt, and every Official Wizard could... could protect one apprentice.”

The blonde girl did not look up, her eyes fixed on her knees. “He used his on me. Of course he did. He always picks me, and you went in there instead, and I let him.”

Maureen watched as Winnie’s fingers played with a fold of the white gauze dress, tugging at it until the fabric creased. The rims of her eyes were red. The scent of crushed herbs and lye soap drifted across the pot of tea, which had long gone cold.

[Tells: shame.] [Not proof.]

Then it added something else, softer in tone. *This is the hug that was missing before the trial. She kept her arms at her side then. Cause: now known.*

“If you hate me for it,” Winnie whispered, “I’d understand.”

Maureen laughed, a low and easy sound. “He raised you from a baby. If he’d chosen anyone else, I’d think less of him.”

Winnie’s shoulders fell, as if releasing some great tension. She gave a wet laugh, then crossed the little room to wrap Maureen in a tight hug. Warmth and the scent of herbs surrounded her, and this time Maureen let herself be held.

*Forgiven?*

*Forgiven.*

Tally didn’t dispute the claim, simply logging it away in its data. *Interesting,* it noted. *This development is seldom encountered. Almost never happens.*

When Winnie was gone, the study fell quiet once more. Maureen turned the cup of cold tea in her hands. One slot. A father’s one slot, spent on his daughter. The sum was clear in Maureen’s mind, and she did not need it written down.

* * *

There were too many empty benches in the dining hall. Every other one stood abandoned, but in the places where the black-robes had sat shoulder to shoulder, eating and laughing and gossiping, there were now bare planks. Empty spaces, cold spots, and the survivors clumped together at the warm end of the hall, as if seeking company.

Maureen picked up the Gasha chef’s offering: veal kidney, two stones’ worth. The gravy was dark and glossy with pepper and butter. She sat down, turning her back to the room, leaving her ears open.

The same name kept bobbing around the next table over. It took several circuits before Maureen caught it. Jack. Jack the Ripper. In a week, the stories of the trial’s survivors had made him famous.

The gangly storyteller leaned forward, his voice dropping confidentially. “And no one knew what he looked like! He just showed up, and he killed Beck. Beck! The one who offed his own Tower’s people, and this Jack put him in the ground like he was nothing.”

[Jack: 11 mentions.]

[Pronoun: he, 11/11.]

*They’re pretty sure,* Maureen told Tally.

Tally sounded smug. *You were very convincing. It helped with the throat.*

Maureen’s knife sank into the kidney, and it parted cleanly for her. The irony sat in her chest like the warmth from wine, so she kept her face pointed at her plate.

“And the Skoll fruit,” said the storyteller. “The thing the leaders wanted. He ate it right off the hounds, right under their noses.”

*He ate two fruits, neither of them off the leaders’ table,* Tally noted.

*Don’t correct the legend.*

*I’m not correcting it. I’m just correcting it for myself. That’s my hobby.*

“He’s ours, you know,” said the freckled girl. “Dark Forest. The wizards read the traces. Undead Wind Arrow, Hand of Negative Energy, all from our own catalogue.”

“Then who is he?” demanded the storyteller. “Not one of us who came back has a total anywhere near his kills. I’ve looked at hands. Nobody’s that high.”

Tally spoke up again, smugly pleased to have something to tell Maureen. *You refused every total in the trial, remember? That’s exactly why no one can find this Jack fellow. They’re looking for someone with a large number. And your total of twelve points gives you the best alibi in the dining hall.*

*Be smug quietly.*

“Barok of the Olivias stood up in council and shouted for Jack’s head,” the freckled girl said in a lower voice. “Every Olivia apprentice died in there. Every single one.”

*Hearsay,* Tally noted, filing it next to its memory of Barok’s flame-wrapped projection in Adrian’s lab on the night when Adrian had burned him halfway through a sentence. *Barok is Jay’s grandfather,* Tally reminded her, as if she might forget.

Tallow smoke hung like fog under the low vault, and the veal kidney had grown lukewarm in its dark pool of butter and pepper. Maureen’s pulse did not move. She used a crust of white bread to wipe a smear of gravy from the edge of her plate. Her hand was perfectly steady, she noticed with some interest.

Maureen allowed one corner of her mouth to turn up. She wondered how the wizards upstairs imagined this Jack the Ripper. What did they picture when they thought about an apprentice who didn’t exist?

She finished every last bite of food, placed her knife and fork side by side on her plate, and left the table to its story. Behind her, the name went round once more.

* * *

The afternoon had been spent in the experiment district, testing out a spell hypothesis against the district's instruments, and now Maureen was on her way back to her rooms. The brass frames with the blue glass panes had hummed as the tiny particles passed through them, and the instruments had gone clickety-click. The corridor she walked along was narrow, and several senior servants suddenly stepped out from a side passage to block her path. One of them held up a sealed order with Steward Augurit’s mark upon it.

“Please follow me.”

The senior servant turned without waiting for her assent. The seal on the order was dark gold, with two beast heads biting each other at the edges, exactly the same as the mark upon the war decree. Maureen’s throat tightened slightly. She tugged down on her robe to straighten it out, and said nothing.

Tally matched the seal against its record of the war decree. It started counting the number of turns they made along their route. *What is this for?* Maureen asked silently.

*No data.* Tally sounded a register colder than she would have liked. *And that, as you know, is the part I dislike.*

The cropped black hair at the base of her neck felt cold in the draught. The senior servant’s soft-soled shoes made not a sound as they crossed the flagstones. Maureen’s were the only footsteps in the corridor.

At a plain door, the senior servant bowed his head slightly before leaving. The door opened, and an apprentice came out. Someone she recognised from the survivors of the trial.

“Good day.”

“Good day.” They bowed to one another, then passed.

*Grey face. Some sweat at the hairline. A bandaged hand cradled close to his chest,* Tally said in Maureen’s head. *Whatever was in there wore on him somewhat. No data on what that could be.*

The room inside was bare. An orb of light floated near the ceiling, bathing all who stood under it in a waxy yellow tint. There was a table, and a single chair of black stone. Several wizards sat around or stood next to the table. All of them looked at Maureen.

She stopped, then bowed to the assembled Official Wizards. She kept her back straight, her arms and legs perfectly controlled. An apprentice before her elders, and properly respectful.

One of the wizards had the young girl’s face and the seaweed-dark hair of Vivian, Richard’s and Lucy’s mentor. She smiled.

“Maureen Olsen? You’re Richard’s friend. He and Lucy have told us a lot about you. Please, sit down. Don’t look so nervous. We just have a few questions for you.”

The black chair was cold against her back. Cold like a cellar step on a winter morning. Vivian’s face might have belonged to a young girl but her eyes did not. To one side of her was another Official Wizard, this one with long, flowing green hair that matched the colour of her robes. She said nothing, and kept one hand out of sight within her sleeve.

*Field: active. Particle sense,* Tally reported as soon as she sat down. *Pattern: reads body. No data on specifics. This is a truth check. For Jack.*

Maureen felt it then, like a thin even film over the seat of the chair, the flat top of the table. It settled on her skin like fine dust. The air itself was listening. Her pulse jumped once, hard, before she could stop it.

*Give me the rhythm.* Tally could only steer what her body could govern, it told her. Pulse, breath, jaw, hands. It would call, she would have to carry. Her pulse jumped again as she contemplated giving up control of her own heartbeat to Tally. *Don’t let go of me,* she told it silently.

*I have you.* Tally’s voice had a colder register when it mattered most.

*I consent.*

Silver Light’s cadence rose beneath her ribs, three long, one short, just as it had in the Brewing State. Tally began calling small corrections. *Pulse down two.* *Jaw loose.* *Breathe out longer.* Her knight-trained body obeyed each instruction.

The questions started with the realm. Where had she gone? Whom had she met? “Points?” the heavy-set wizard asked, his chair creaking under him.

“Twelve, sir. I hid when I could, and fought when they cornered me.”

Tally’s withheld list scrolled past at the edge of Maureen’s vision. The Seal. The grenades. Jack. Freya’s sword. The eggs.

Maureen kept her account straight. Mid-pack. Twelve points. Lucky. A bit dull.

*I am a bit dull,* she told Tally.

*You are a great many things. Dull is the one you do best at faking.*

The thin wizard’s eyes went to Maureen’s hair. “That’s short.”

“A fire spell got closer than I liked, sir. It singed. I cut it off so I wouldn’t smell it.”

As Maureen spoke, Tally called: out longer, hands still. The film on Maureen’s skin was just as even as before, no ripple that she could feel. The orb overhead hummed faintly. The heavy-set wizard’s chair creaked every time he shifted. The questions were mild, placed one at a time like stones on a board, each in its correct place.

Then they asked about the Dark Forest: her mentor, her duties in the lab, the potions she crafted and sold, and to whom she sold them. The mission to Fasol. Gentle questions, but between them they mapped out her years here as neatly as any surveyor.

Vivian tilted her head. “Richard says you made Third-Class. He’s very proud of you.”

*You told Richard and Lucy to tell them that,* Tally reminded her. *Use pharmacology. Give them the dull thing.*

So Maureen talked about potions. Success rates of Recovery concoctions, the timing of separation ingredients, the smell of Adrian’s Holan grubs. After a while, Vivian’s smile became fixed. “An apothecary, then. Thank you for your time, Maureen. That is all.”

Maureen stood up from her seat. She bowed her head to each of them in turn, then left the room, pulling the door shut behind her. Tally was able to hear the voices through the wood, by sound alone.

“Well?” Vivian asked.

A woman’s voice, tired. “Low suspicion. Probably not him.”

*Him,* Tally repeated.

A few steps down the corridor, the even prickle vanished from Maureen’s skin. Her knees buckled once, hard, before she regained control and let them shake. Her palm rested against the cold black stone wall until the quivering stopped.

Tally eased off on the cadence, letting Maureen’s own rhythm creep back in. *Good work,* it told her. *You did the work.*

*We did the work,* Maureen told it, and pushed herself away from the wall.

* * *

Two days later the laboratory looked exactly like it always did.

The burner ticked. The test tube rested within its heatless black flame. There was a smell, sharp enough to sting the inside of her nose.

Adrian pushed up his glasses, sliding a bundle of cloth across the long stone bench. “I told you all,” he said, his voice as calm and flat as ever. “Whoever came out of that realm would be well rewarded. This is yours.”

The black cloth was heavy, woven with a thick thread. It was cold too, as if it had been sitting for a very long time in some vault. Maureen bowed her head to Adrian before she untied the string.

There were two cards of low-grade magic stones within, along with a book, a scroll, a small purple vial, and a scattering of minor materials and dried plants.

Tally weighed the magic stone cards by eye. [Cards: +2. 1,000 each.]

It glanced over the materials, but none of them were new to its files. She let them be.

Maureen’s fingers brushed against the book and the scroll, and tiny rune symbols lifted themselves into the air from their pages. They hovered for a moment, flickering.

The symbols winked like sparks thrown off by a forge, except these gave off no heat. Instead, a faint cold radiated from them, touching Maureen’s knuckles.

“The scroll is a spell template for an official spell,” Adrian explained. “The book is a meditation method.” A brief pause. “The Dark Forest’s junk meditation method.”

*Junk,* Tally noted. *He used the word to her face.* Tally made note of it, filing it next to everything else he’d told her about junk methods and their cages.

If Maureen had been a regular apprentice, she would have used the two magic stone cards plus her savings to purchase Finlan Potion. Then she would have used the spell template and the junk method to finally become the Official Wizard she always dreamed of being.

Maureen didn’t need any of it.

She kept her hand flat on the cover of the book. Her face was impassive, showing neither pleasure nor disappointment at the gifts.

Then she turned her attention to the small vial. The silvery liquid inside moved on its own despite nobody touching it. It glittered and sparkled like sand beneath a river illuminated by starlight. She looked up at Adrian with her eyebrows raised in question.

“Silver-Trace Ink,” he said. “Also part of your reward. A good help when you advance to Official Wizard, and a valuable item for any apprentice to have.”

Maureen reached out and took the vial into her hand. The glass was cool against her skin, but the silver within moved slowly against it. Deep under her breastbone, the lines of the rune she had laid began to tilt in the direction of the vial, like plants leaning towards a window.

[Junk method: 1 book.]

[Spell template: 1 scroll.]

[Silver-Trace Ink: 1.]

[Minai: 3 of 4 in hand.]

The inventory update popped up in her vision. Tally also noted that it couldn’t read the contents of the book until Maureen flipped through the pages.

*Against my ribs,* Maureen told Tally. *Not in the hollow.*

*Yes,* Tally agreed.

Adrian kept his eyes on Maureen’s hands as he spoke. “Barok wants this Jack dead. He says it is his uncle Philip’s word. Philip Olivia is Elemental Solid State, a hair away from Second-Tier. The hunt will be gentle.” He paused. “I asked for that.”

[Adrian: eyes on your hands, not your face. Tells: none. Not proof.]

*Does he know?* Maureen asked Tally.

*No data,* Tally answered.

“They might offer the survivors a chance to choose an advanced meditation method, if they’re within quota,” Adrian continued as if he were discussing nothing important at all. “And the war with Hader Castle, the Blue Ring, and the Mist Labyrinth might not happen at all. Something else has come up.”

Maureen thanked him and bowed. By the time she had reached the end of the corridor, the vial of Silver-Trace Ink had warmed against her ribs, but she still wasn’t sure whether her mentor knew.

Tally didn’t know either.
