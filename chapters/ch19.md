It was a quiet time on board the tortoise-drawn vessel. Both the old black-robes and the new seeds kept themselves to themselves, wary of the beast they travelled upon, and of Evans. The barnacled shell of the giant tortoise rose above the bow, and with every slow breath the whole deck would lift under Maureen Olsen’s feet then settle back down again.

One day, walking along one of the ship’s lower corridors, Maureen had seen an old black-robe shove a new seed up against a bulkhead. The next morning the seed had a split lip. Otherwise it was quiet.

Maureen kept up her routine of rune meditation in her cabin, Silver Light breathing at the rail every dawn, her fingertips resting against her sternum, followed by sword drills in some empty stretch of the lower deck.

One morning, as Maureen stood at the rail and breathed, a pale blue line ran across her display - [Silver Light: three long, one short]. Forty-two seconds. The tortoise breathes roughly four times a minute. You’ve fallen into its rhythm.

The tortoise is a better teacher than Scarlette was, Maureen responded silently to the chip buried in her brain. I have learned much from it.

I know, said Tally, a little smugly. I’m logging it as faculty.

 Tally’s display opened before Maureen’s eyes.

[Spirit Runes: 8/12]

[Middle Knight: Strength 7.5, Agility 7.7, Constitution 7.8]

Your progress continues, Tally said, its voice warm in Maureen’s head. Though it is slowing somewhat. It sounded vaguely put out about this fact.

A new line appeared on her display, flickering into life.

[Plan six: blocked. Fanged black wolf eyes: no data on location.]

I know, Maureen told it silently, making no sound with her mouth.

Yes, well, I’m keeping count of the days anyway. She felt the count tick over behind her eyes. Brine crusted the rails, leaving them white. Tar clung to the soles of her boots. The wooden planks beneath her feet smelled like old salt fish.

Later, while she drilled on an empty stretch of the lower deck, Richard watched her go through the same cut for the hundredth time. He laughed at her, telling her that she was a block of wood, far too stiff for her own good.

Maureen only smiled.

Block of wood? Tally offered in her head. Shall I log that as an official description next to ‘Wizard Seed Olsen’?

Don’t you dare, Maureen told it silently.

Filed, said Tally, sounding a little smug. A pale blue flicker passed behind Maureen’s eyes.

At night the hull creaked around her bunk and the great tortoise’s breath came up through the timbers like the tide, slow and regular.

* * *

The morning’s ration sat in her bowl, a heel of hard bread and a strip of salt fish. It weighed practically nothing in her hand.

Tally considered it against yesterday’s portion, first by the feel in her hand, then by what was in the bowl. [Ration: 46% of prior day.]

Anthony came down the corridor, frowning. “Have you noticed?” he asked Maureen and Richard. “Today’s ration is less than half of yesterday’s.”

He still smelled of soap, but the thread of bright gold that edged the cuffs of his white shirt had been dulled by salt.

Tally finished its calculation before he even finished his sentence, but Maureen didn’t say anything.

Let him tell me, Maureen told Tally silently, keeping her face perfectly blank.

Richard nodded, looking puzzled. “I thought it was just my bowl that was coming up short.”

“Exactly,” said Anthony. “I was just about to speak with Lady Emily about it.” The seed from the Principality of Gasha had perfect manners, but there was an intensity in his eyes.

“What do you make of it?” Anthony asked quietly.

Richard scratched his head. “I don’t know.”

“What do you think, Anthony?” Maureen asked.

The seed considered for a moment. He glanced at Maureen. “It is hard to guess a wizard’s intentions,” he said finally, pressing his lips together in a smile that did not reach his eyes.

That man needs allies, Tally told Maureen. He will swallow this refusal.

More footsteps sounded overhead. There seemed to be more people moving around above them, and a low murmur penetrated through the wooden planks of the ceiling.

Maureen straightened her black robe and drew up her hood. “We should go up and have a look.”

* * *

On deck every seed had been shorted. Many had received no food at all. The seeds stood around in small knots, murmuring to each other. Empty bowls hung from their hands, and the gulls circled and screamed overhead. The timbers beneath the seeds’ feet rose and fell with the swell, the sound of hundreds of feet scraping against the wet wood sounding like a strange accompaniment to the sea.

[Count: about 540, by sight], Tally announced, dividing the mass into different sectors and performing a quick count before superimposing a rough tally over their heads.

A big, broad-built youth with bright golden hair stepped out from one of the groups. “Hey!” he yelled up to Evans. “Why’d you cut our food?”

[Match: blond youth, Aaron’s beating], Tally announced, flagging the face of a tall and thin youth standing at the speaker’s left shoulder.

So that’s Rheas, Maureen told Tally silently.

So that’s the boss, Tally responded.

Rheas’s eyes scanned the deck, finding Maureen’s hooded figure across the crowd. They lingered on her for a moment before moving on.

“Shut your mouth,” said a cold female voice from the upper rail. “The lord Evans is not for the likes of you to question.”

The big youth’s face flushed red. Before he could respond, a quiet voice spoke out. “Enough, Emily.” Dark energy particles began to gather into a form on Evans’s upper level. The Shadow Hunter smiled down at the seeds from the rail, his narrow eyes slits in his pale face, his lips a thin line.

[Spike: known source].

It’s the same reading as Scarlette’s, Tally said quietly.

I know, Maureen responded silently.

And I still can’t tell you how much stronger he is.

Maureen did so, reading it in the flinch the Dark Forest apprentice made as she stepped back from the rail.

She went still. Not a twitching muscle or a shift of her stance gave away her tension. Her shoulders were loose and relaxed, but ready to snap into action at any moment. She kept her breathing shallow, not wanting the motion of her chest to give away her preparation.

“You’ve all come?” Evans asked. “How nice. Because now, I want to say something to all of you.” His voice was quiet, but carried along the deck easily, and a sudden silence fell. The gulls continued to wheel overhead, but on the main deck there was no sound. Maureen could smell the wet timber and tar beneath her feet, and hear the slap of the waves against the barnacled shell below the bow. Evans’s escort stood motionless along the rails, their black plate shining dully. “It’s been so calm and dull lately, hasn’t it? Let’s play a game.”

“There are five hundred and forty-seven of you, all told. For now, I will provide you with enough food for two hundred of you a day.”

Evans held up one finger, and his quiet smile grew even stranger. For now. Perhaps later he would provide food for a hundred... fifty... thirty... or none.

Tally didn’t need to be asked to begin its calculations. Three hundred and forty-seven would go without food today. If the number fell to thirty, they’d have five hundred and seventeen left over every day.

Maureen’s hands went still at her sides. Stop, she told Tally silently. And it did, its calculations halting midstream.

But then, in a small voice, Tally spoke again. Maureen Olsen, you are in the two hundred . . . if you wish to be.

Five hundred faces turned upward, staring at Evans standing at the rail above them. There was no sound, not even from the birds overhead. Only the sea lapping against the hull and the slow, deep breath of the tortoise rising through the deck penetrated the silence.

Evans tilted his head, looking down at the mass of people gathered below him. “You all seem to have opinions. Say them.” His voice was quiet, almost conversational. “Don’t worry, I’ve always been a very merciful person. Truly.”

Richard’s fingers locked onto Maureen’s sleeve. She could feel their grip, trembling slightly. She remembered the burst seed on the road. No one spoke.

Evans laughed softly, the sound carrying along the deck. He disintegrated into a swarm of dark particles, but his quiet voice remained hanging in the air. “No objections, then, future wizards? Very well. The game has begun.”

Emily sneered down at them before turning and disappearing back into the inner cabin. The black-plate knights stood motionless as statues, and slowly, the seeds began to drift away from one another.

* * *

Maureen sat down on the narrow plank that served as her bed, and let out a long, slow breath. The days ahead were going to be interesting. Many mouths, not enough food. Whatever happened on this ship, she had no doubt it would go far beyond anyone’s guesses.

She laid the Cross Sword across her knees, and slowly drew a damp cloth along its blade.

The wet cloth is removing the blue coating along with the grime. Don’t forget to glove up before you recoat.

Nag, nag, nag, Maureen thought back silently.

The hare would disagree, Tally said.

Maureen’s hands moved slowly and evenly up and down the steel, watching each stroke turn the cloth a shade darker grey.

She slipped on the leather glove and ran it down the edge of the sword, reapplying the faintly greasy sheen from the blue vial. Light from the porthole danced along the steel as the deck rose and fell beneath her, and through the wall, Maureen heard the creak of Richard’s bunk.

Maureen reached over and stretched the cord with the harness bell across the door again.

Logged. Interesting days ahead.
