“Sit on that stump over there,” Maureen said, pointing to an old mossy stump. “Unroll a blank contract from your satchel. It’s a Jadeleaf Ring trade form. All the apprentices out here carry one.”

Joanna nodded, walked over to the stump, and sat down. Her own satchel lay beside her, and she opened it up, pulling out a rolled up sheet of blank paper.

Tally studied the document as Joanna unrolled it upon her lap. It was indeed a standard Jadeleaf Ring contract form. Identical to the one Jay had used for his Olivia scroll.

Joanna signed it the old-fashioned way. A brand of light lifted from her forehead, and the scroll began to burn until it was a pile of green-edged ash.

Tally laid the burn beside her records of Jay’s Olivia scroll and the Dark Forest vault contract. Same form, same seal, same kind of burn.

*And contracts have ways around contracts,* Tally reminded her.

*Yes, I know,* Maureen replied silently. *That’s why there’s going to be a fire inside this one too.*

Maureen reached forward, placing two fingers just below the base of Joanna’s collarbone. A thread of black-red flame slid beneath the young woman’s breastbone and went dark. It was the same fire that had swallowed Barok. Joanna had watched that fire consume a wizard, and now she stopped breathing.

Instinctively, Joanna placed her palm flat against where the fire rested inside her. Perhaps she expected some heat. There was none. Her knuckles were white, though, and soon her breath returned in small, shallow sips. Like someone afraid to wake a sleeping dog curled up in her chest.

Tally pinned a small ember marker to the overlay above Joanna’s chest.

[Seed: dormant.]

[Upkeep: none observed.]

[Ignites: on your will.]

*Could you light that, Tally?*

*I could. I won’t unless you tell me to. And I would like that written down too, please.*

“Uh... Wizard Olsen?” Joanna said, trying to be useful. “Couldn’t we exchange our wizard brands so we can send word to one another back in Emilia?”

Maureen’s wrist itched where Daniel’s mark still rested beneath Adrian’s brand.

*No,* Tally said. *You can’t do that. A wizard brand goes one way only. Who was it who taught us that?*

*Daniel,* Maureen answered silently.

*Yes, Daniel. One-way. You call, she answers, but nothing runs back the other way.*

Maureen reached out and pressed a mark into the inside of Joanna’s wrist. There was a momentary cold sting, a faint ring of light, and then it was done. Now she could call upon Joanna, speak to her, and Joanna would only be able to answer.

Joanna looked at the brand, but said nothing.

They began walking again, heading west. The beeches spread their branches overhead, letting a grey light filter through the bare tops. Joanna walked ahead of Maureen. Every few steps, she glanced back over her shoulder, giving the look of a woman who had gone on a study trip and returned on a leash.

“It was only supposed to be a journey,” she muttered under her breath.

In the hollow, still hidden from sight of the camp, Maureen stopped walking. “From here on,” she said to Joanna, “my name is Richard. You will call me nothing else.”

*Sentimental,* Tally’s voice noted from within, where only Maureen could hear it.

*Familiar.*

*Both.*

*Again.*

Maureen nodded to herself, and cast Mimicry under Tally’s pale blue muscle map, which hovered invisibly over her own face and down her throat so that only her eyes could see it. Her jaw softened, her chin receded, and the rest of her features lost their definition, becoming mousy and unremarkable. She was once more the plain young man she’d been on the northern road. She left her black hair cropped short as it was.

[Mana: −0.4.]

The sensation of ants marching beneath her skin, then a dull ache settling into the bone. Joanna watched with a hand to her own throat, though she seemed unaware that she’d done so. The features on Maureen’s face moved like warm wax.

Maureen cleared her throat. “Richard,” she said.

*You’re drifting, Maureen,* Tally’s voice said in her head. *Go lower.*

“Richard,” she said again, and this time the reshaped throat gave a low, flat voice. A young man’s voice.

*Good.*

Then Maureen reached inside herself, grasping her Official Wizard’s aura and field, willing them to shrink by force. It felt as if she were closing her hand around a lamp, compressing the light until it was gone. Nothing about an Official Wizard would show now.

*Apprentice,* Tally’s voice reported from inside. *As far as I can tell, you read as an apprentice on the outside, too. I’ll be watching those eyes, though. That Pankora crimson can flicker through when you get angry.*

*Fine.*

Maureen could feel wizard particles ahead, crowding through the trees. “There they are,” she told Joanna.

Joanna’s expression brightened with simple relief. “Yes, Lord Richard. They’re just through there.”

Through the tree trunks came the sounds of horses whickering, a girl’s high laugh, and the clink of glass against glass. People who’d never been afraid upon the road.

* * *

The glade beyond did not look like a camp, but like a picnic. Lacquered carriages stood around the clearing, each bearing a small enamel pin on its door to identify the family. Awnings of silk had been stretched between the vehicles, and one groom was brushing a grey mare to a shine. The smell of spilled wine drifted out from the clearing to mingle with the scents of horse and harness polish. The sunlight glittered off the vehicles’ polished surfaces.

A blonde girl spotted Joanna and ran across the grass, calling out, “Sister!”

Joanna’s shoulders lowered for the first time that morning. “Christine!”

Christine was beautiful, even more so than her sister. Her hair was a deep, rich gold, and her skin was as white as milk. She wore a dress whose bodice was laced tightly to show off her figure. As she crossed the glade, every head among the young men turned to follow her progress, like flowers turning to face the sun.

She skidded to a stop in front of her sister, looking at Maureen with bright blue eyes. “Sister, who is this?” There was an edge to the question, as if Joanna had brought home something she wasn’t supposed to have.

“Richard,” Joanna said, a bit too quickly. “A friend. We ran into each other in the woods.” Maureen nodded once, letting her plain features do the talking.

*Friend,* Tally noted, picking up the faint jump in Joanna’s pulse. *Her lying needs practice.*

*So does mine,* Maureen thought back.

Christine’s eyes swept over Maureen’s travel-worn coat, her plain and forgettable face, and the faint aura of an apprentice wizard. They slid away from Maureen, as if she were just another chair or table.

*I’ve seen that look before,* Maureen thought. *I’ve just always seen it from the other side.* The Duke’s guests had always looked right through the maids who served them in Skran’s halls. Now, from the other end, she felt . . . nothing. Just a little, dry recognition, like finding the same crack in two pieces of broken pottery.

A knot of young noblemen and women waved, dressed in their finest, calling out “Christine! Christine!”

Joanna exchanged two quick sentences with her sister, then Christine dashed off to join her friends. Within a few minutes, the cheers of the group floated back across the glade.

Joanna turned back to Maureen, her face pale. “I’m sorry, Lord Richard,” Joanna said. “She didn’t mean to—” She was clearly afraid her sister had just insulted a wizard who could burn people.

Maureen Olsen didn’t even turn her head toward the sound of the cheering youths. “The carriage,” she said. “As I told you. Don’t forget, I want the best one.”

These people were nothing to Maureen, except for the fact that they could provide her with a ride to Emilia and its wizard academy.

*You could have added a please,* Tally noted. *It wouldn’t have hurt.*

*Joanna would only have taken it as a request,* Maureen answered silently.

In less than an hour, Maureen had the widest and best-sprung carriage in the caravan. She tied her black horn-scaled horse behind the vehicle, then hung a sack of raw beef from the horse’s saddle. She’d picked up the meat in Peaceridge.

The horse ripped into the beef, the smell of blood and hot iron rising like steam from the scales on its flanks. Two young noblemen, grooming their horses for the journey, stopped their brushing to stare. One man stepped back, bumping right into a wagon wheel.

*I think that your horse is the most frightening member of this party,* Tally said. *At least, from the standpoint of these people.*

Maureen climbed into her carriage and pulled the curtains shut.

* * *

The caravan rolled west through the green country. They passed hedgerows and ditches that glinted with irrigation water, and at the noon halt, the noblemen pulled out bottles of wine rather than ale, drinking beneath silk awnings while their laughter carried further than the creaking of the wagon wheels.

Behind the drawn curtains where no one could see the Pouch, Maureen pulled out the two Official spell models she’d purchased with Adrian’s access before leaving the Dark Forest. She also took out the Dark Forest template scroll.

The books were colder than any Rank Zero tome. They weighed more than they should have, given their size. When she set her palm against the cover of either book, it hummed like a struck bell. The carriage smelled of ink and lamp oil.

As an Official Wizard, Maureen had to engrave each of the spell models directly into her own mind. She began with the first, building the lattice line by line. It was slow going at first. It reminded her of the time when she had to build an entire reagent train from scratch on Jing Sheng’s bench.

*Can I hold this half for you while you work on the next?* Tally asked when Maureen finished the first half of the model. *I’ll keep it steady so you can move on to the next part without having to rebuild everything from the start.*

Maureen considered, then nodded her consent. [Holding. −0.1/min.]

*It’s not that hard,* Tally said. *I’m just keeping it from falling over.*

*You’ve done this before?* Maureen asked silently.

*Since we got to the manor,* Tally said. *Quietly.* *You didn’t think that I was just going to sit around inside your head, did you?*

It made the process go much faster. The lattice remained as solid as spun glass in Tally’s grasp while Maureen worked on the next half. Because of the mana cost of the process, Maureen had to work in short sessions, then meditate between to regenerate what she’d spent.

Between modeling sessions, Maureen read through a thick stack of Official Wizard material. It covered everything a new wizard was expected to know—from how to manage a field to how to interact with members of one’s own order.

Pen scratching against paper, lamp swinging gently as the carriage rolled down the road, rocked back and forth by the movement of the vehicle—it was the closest thing to contentment Maureen had felt since leaving the Dark Forest. Work in her hands, Tally in her head, and a curtain between her and the rest of the world.

Joanna came to the carriage several times a day, with water for the pitcher or fresh cushions or some other excuse for asking if there was anything Richard wanted.

The caravan had noticed Joanna’s attentions, and the noble youths began watching the apprentice’s door. The sour remarks began outside her window.

“I hear Richard is in the best carriage,” said one of the loudest of them.

“An apprentice?” said another. “Joanna’s lapdog.”

“Let him step out here once, and we’ll teach the little snot some manners with steel.”

From the way the others called to these two, Maureen learned their names: Lawrence and Berry. From their conversation, she learned a good deal more. One day when Joanna visited her, Maureen asked, “Aren’t they afraid of you? Aren’t they afraid of any wizard?”

“In the west of the Passise, many of the noble houses are descended from wizards,” Joanna replied. “Bloodline nobility. There are so many families with wizard blood that it’s hard to separate noble and wizard. These boys have grown up at wizards’ tables.”

Like Duke Skran’s careful smile, as he’d welcomed Scarlette to his court. Like the Eastern nobility, sweating as a Mystic turned his eyes upon them. Here, a boy wearing his family’s pin on his shoulder could laugh in the face of a wizard because his great-grandfather had been one.

Tally filed away the pins and crests of the various noble families, but only under the spoken names of their wearers. She had no recorded sigils against which to compare them. Then Tally opened a new column. Insults, Lawrence 6. Insults, Berry 4.

*No, Tally, stop counting those. I don’t need to know.*

*I am counting for evidence, Maureen. In case you decide to kill them.*

Maureen rolled her eyes. *That’s not going to happen.*

Tally also kept track of how often Maureen’s pen stopped its scratching across the page when the sound of Lawrence laughing passed outside her window. *Four times today.*

*The ink dried, Tally. I had to wait for it to flow again.*

*Disputed.*

When Berry rapped on the panel of Maureen’s carriage and shouted, “Come out here, Richard!” Tally spoke up. *The crimson is beginning to show in your eyes, Maureen.*

Maureen immediately lowered her eyes to her page until Tally told her they were black again.

In the early evening, when the camp began to glow with the light of lamps shining through silken walls, the smell of fine wine and roast fowl drifting through the air, and the sound of Christine’s laugh rising above the voices of the other young men, Maureen’s own carriage remained a pool of darkness at the edge of the light. Its curtains were drawn.

* * *

Tally’s chime sounded in Maureen’s head while the carriage was still quiet.

*Joanna. By density. Alone. Pulse rate one hundred ten.*

*Let her in,* Maureen responded to Tally silently, not even lifting her pen from the page.

Then there came a soft tapping at the carriage door.

“Come in, Joanna.”

The door creaked open, admitting a breath of cold night air into the warmth of the carriage.

Joanna climbed the steps. She was wearing a black robe, plain and unadorned. Her hair fell loose around her shoulders, and she kept her hands knotted together at her waist with such force that the knuckles gleamed white in the lamplight. Through the gap behind her came another burst of laughter from the noble tents outside.

“Lord Maureen—” Joanna began, and the name hung in the little space filled with light.

Tally flashed the word red across Maureen’s sight before she had even fully heard it. Maureen looked up at Joanna and the Jadeleaf Ring apprentice swallowed hard.

“Lord Richard.” The word sounded forced out of her mouth.

*That is the first time Joanna has made that slip, Maureen,* said Tally’s dry voice. *I recommend a gag for her when she speaks.*

“I . . .” Joanna looked down at her hands, which were still knotted together as though they were trying to choke each other. “I just wanted to apologise. Lawrence and Berry have been rude these past days, and I hope you do not mind. They are young and sometimes forget themselves.”

“It doesn’t matter,” Maureen answered. The pen did not stop its scratching.

The pen scratched away, and Joanna’s eyes kept flicking toward the curtain. Toward the tents outside, where Christine laughed with the same young men who glared at this carriage during the day.

Then Joanna dropped to her knees on the wooden boards. Her robe remained modestly over her body, but pooled around her as she knelt. She laid her hands palm-up in front of her, open. It was the traditional oath-taking posture of the western houses.

“I swear to you,” she said, “everything I am or ever will be is yours, Lord Richard. Sworn.” She swallowed once more. “In exchange for this gift, I beg that you overlook anything Christine may have done or said. I beg it in her name, for she would not come to you herself, but I fear for her soul.”

Tally read Joanna’s shaking hands and the pulse at the side of her throat. *No feint observed,* Tally said.

*That’s not proof,* Maureen thought.

Something sour turned over in the pit of her stomach. A person offering herself as an entry in someone else’s ledger. The purse placed next to her plate at the Duke’s table. The terms the Walkers used, the price Lilith had carried. A hundred and fifty years. Eighty percent.

Maureen put her pen down for the first time. She arched an eyebrow. “How interesting. If it matters to you so much, have Christine come and apologise to me herself. Or better yet, both of you on your knees some evening, and I will consider the matter.”

Colour flooded into Joanna’s face, right up to the tips of her ears. “Lord Richard...” she said, and had nothing further to add.

Maureen waved her away, closed her eyes and returned to the lattice in her head, as if the woman kneeling before her was already gone.

*That was cruel,* Tally said.

*It was a door,* Maureen replied silently. *She can walk through it or not.*

*I have logged it as a door,* Tally said.

Joanna left the carriage wrapped tightly in the black robe. Through the carriage panel Maureen heard her stop on the step outside and let out a long breath that was almost a laugh - relief and helplessness combined - before her footsteps faded away across the wet grass.

* * *

Over the next few days the contempt became less guarded. Christine’s crowd laughed behind their gloves at Richard, and Lawrence would spit in the dust by the side of her carriage wheel as she climbed down when they made a halt. Tally kept a running total. Lawrence 11, Berry 7.

*Just in case,* she added.

*I’m thinking about it,* Maureen replied silently.

Tally ran through the costs all on her own now, voice warm but level, no judgment. *Two boys on a quiet night with no witnesses. Trivial. Joanna’s grief. Not trivial.*

Maureen weighed it up as she would weigh any reagent, considering it coldly, letting it sit on an unshaking scale inside her head. Her pulse did not move, her hand did not tremble. She remembered the feeling of Lauren’s neck under her hand. The next camp after dark, she decided.

But that afternoon the caravan ground to a jerking halt, and shouts came from the lead carriages. Maureen drew back the curtain. The dust was hanging gold in the sun, still low in the western sky, and the horses were blowing and stamping, and the noble youths were climbing down from their carriages and beginning to drift towards something on the verge.

A girl sat on the dry grass at the edge of the lane, hugging her knees. The caravan had rumbled to a stop in a loose circle around her. She looked to be about sixteen or seventeen. Under the dirt she was beautiful. She wore ragged grey clothing, and her feet were bare and grey with dust. Her eyes were clear and dark, and they blinked and blinked like a fawn’s, startled by the sudden attention. Even Lawrence’s voice sank to a soft murmur as he walked towards her.

Tally reached out with her all-round sense of particles, and the sense came back with a simple message. There was no particle reaction, not even the faint warmth Maureen sensed from other people: [Source: none detected. Visual: present.]

*Something is there that my sense says is not. I do not like that sentence.*

“Where have you come from?” Lawrence asked as he bent down and looked at the ragged girl. “Are you hurt? Are you lost?”

The girl pressed her lips tightly together and shook her head. Christine stood at the back of the circle, blonde as ever, and obviously unused to being ignored as the young men bent eagerly forward, their faces full of concern as they addressed the stranger.

Maureen waited for her to answer, but the silence drew out. One of the horses stamped on the hard road, its bit jangling a little in protest at the sudden stop. Maureen leaned on her carriage doorway, her face still plain and unassuming, wearing Richard’s borrowed features. She watched the girl’s eyes. Not once did they glance in her direction.

“I... I’m Alice,” the girl said eventually, her voice small and timid, like a bird that had just been startled by an owl. “I came to find someone.”

*I have another Alice on file,* Tally’s quiet voice sounded in Maureen’s head. *The first was the ship girl who wouldn’t touch her food until you put the ration in front of her.*

*Hush,* Maureen said silently. Tally fell silent, and Maureen’s display stayed blank.

“I came to find my sister,” Alice continued. “Maureen... Maureen Olsen.”

Maureen went perfectly still. Her plain, mild face didn’t change expression, but her breath stopped in her chest. Someone new, here on this empty road, had spoken her name. Her real name. Her grandfather’s name.

Maureen’s pulse leapt on her display, and Tally’s voice spoke again. [Hold the face. Look down. Do not speak.]

Maureen obeyed all three instructions. At the edge of the crowd, Maureen saw Joanna turn her head briefly towards the carriage before turning away.

Alice hugged her knees tighter with her arms, and dropped her eyes back to the dust at her feet. “I came to find her... so we can go home together.”
