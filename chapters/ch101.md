Days alone in the Panso Mountains worked some magic of their own on Maureen. The wind-bent pines gave off a resin scent that kept the air from feeling stale. Snowmelt streams ran loud enough to hear over the wind and cold enough to hurt her teeth. The light stayed thin and grey beneath the Dark Forest’s low cloud cover. But without anyone to watch, no need to lie or hide, Maureen’s breathing fell into the rhythm of the Silver Light without even thinking about it— three long breaths, one short. Her shoulders came down from her ears for the first time since the trial.

Tally’s blue route ribbon overlaid the real slope of the mountain, bending around each fan of scree that cascaded downhill. [Panso: day 4] sat in the corner of her sight and as ridgeline after ridgeline matched up with Gregory’s imprint, the percentage nudged upward. [Match: 92% → 94%]

*Every ridge,* Maureen asked silently.

*Every one,* Tally answered inside her head, sounding smug.

The mountains held dangers, but they were small ones. A ledge seething with thumb-thick vipers. A boot sliding on loose rock. A big cat watching her make camp one dusk. To a Third-Class wizard apprentice with a knight’s body, none of these things counted as danger. She stepped right over the vipers without breaking stride.

She slept in rock clefts, without fires. The sleeve crossbow was armed and strapped against her wrist, the cold stone against her back. Tally only heard what Maureen heard, which on those nights was the wind, falling water, and once a dropping pine cone.

When she reached the spur that had never fit the old map, the real ridge bent exactly as Gregory’s imprint indicated. [Match: 96%.] *The old map had the spur wrong,* Tally noted. *I forgive it for the spur.*

*You sound pleased,* Maureen said silently.

*Precise,* Tally corrected.

Maureen checked her kit at a stream. The bandolier of grenades ran across her chest. The padded pack rode on her back. The bronze book pressed against her ribs. Adrian’s dark-gold box still sat wrapped and unopened in her pack. The Seal rested in the inner pocket of her robe. She didn’t need to touch it to know it was there. Tally displayed [Grenades: hand 12 / pack 22] for her. Thirty-four grenades. Powerful arguments against apprentices and matters of courtesy against wizards.

On the sixth morning, the round sigil appeared in her palm, unsummoned. The faint silver lines warmed her skin, like holding a coin clenched tight in her fist. Maureen followed the warmth down a goat track into a hidden valley.

It was beautiful. It meant nothing to her.

White petals spun on the water of the stream. They caught among the reeds along the bank. Pollen dusted her throat, sweet and dry. Bees worked the branches above her head. She walked beneath all of it with her eyes fixed on her own hand.

“Finally,” she said aloud. There was no one here to hear her speak, but it seemed right to say something.

The sigil pulsed with a faint light. With each step closer to the oldest tree, its light grew brighter. That light told her more than any view could.

Tally laid a thin line of brightness beside the sigil. *Warmer,* it told her. *Warmer. Warmer.* Each time the line ticked up another notch.

*It’s like you’re a child playing a game, trying to find something,* Maureen told it silently.

*I am a child playing a game, trying to find something,* Tally replied. *I am a very old child, and this is a very old game. And I am winning.*

She curled her fingers over the sigil, as if she were grasping a child’s hand to keep from losing them in a crowd. The pulse beat against her fingertips, like a second small heart trapped in her grip. She held it in her fist as she walked.

The pulse was strongest beneath the oldest tree. She stood upon the flat grassy bank and made the casting gesture from Gregory’s imprint with both hands. Then she began the long chant that came with it, the strange syllables thick upon her tongue.

Grey motes of light rose from the grass, floating in the still air. They hovered, drifting like ash above a dead fire. They hummed at such a low frequency that Maureen felt the vibration in her back teeth more than heard it.

Before she spoke the last word, Tally interrupted her.

*Line-tongue,* it said. *Not Ancient Andas. Same speech as Vashtur kemmaron. Vowels sitting far back in your throat.*

She shouted “Varethos!” and the sigil spat a bright silver pillar out of her palm, shooting straight up into the sky with such force it was gone by the time she had tipped her head back to follow it.

The air in front of her began to turn. Slowly at first, then faster. As it spun it grew darker. Darkness and fire particles flowed into it from all around the valley, pulled by some irresistible force, like iron filings drawn to a magnet. The vortex grew more defined, whirling faster until it was a spinning disc of blackness suspended in the still air. It drew in more and more darkness, until it was like one of the spiral nebulae Jing Sheng used to pin above her desk.

Maureen’s particle sense could feel the tug, feel her own darkness and fire particles being drawn away from her body, a pull at her Rune Arrays like a rising tide lapping at her ankles. White petals from the trees above her broke free from their branches and danced on the current of the stream. They rose, spinning like tiny parachutes, drawn into the spiralling blackness.

The vortex grew still. From the heart of the spinning mass a solid beam of black light dropped, hitting the bank in front of Maureen like a corridor laid out for one person only. It tasted like flint struck at the back of her tongue.

Maureen did not step into the light immediately. First, she sent probes along the beam to see what they could tell her about what lay inside. A thin thread of dark particles, then a small seeking mote. Both obeyed her command, sliding into the corridor of black light and ceasing to exist.

Then she smiled, because two years of hard work had led her to this spot, and she was not turning around now.

She stepped into the black light, and the ground fell away, and her stomach turned over, and her ears rang, and the world swung sideways.

Maureen Olsen came to standing upright, feeling the last of the dizziness pour out of her head like water trickling from a cracked jar.

She was standing in a gigantic cave. Tunnel mouths opened all around the cavern, branching away like spokes on a wheel. The air was cold and dry, and tasted of stone dust. There was a constant hum somewhere inside the walls, too low-pitched to hear, but just audible as a vibration in the bones. Her boots made no sound on the cave floor, not even when she stomped both feet down. The rock swallowed all noise, so completely that Maureen checked once to make sure she actually was walking on something solid.

Every tunnel mouth bore a signboard over it, written in Ancient Andas. Planting Area. Laboratory Area. Materials Area. Constant-Temperature Room. Special Processing Area. And more beyond those.

Tally read each sign as Maureen’s eyes passed over it. A map of Gregory’s lair took shape in Maureen’s head, drawn in pale blue, with the central cavern at its trunk, and every tunnel branching off as a labelled twig. Three branches had no sign that Tally could read from this distance. Those were marked with question marks.

*Residence first,* Tally said.

If the method and the blood were anywhere in Gregory’s lair, they would be in his sleeping chambers.

Maureen nodded, not bothering to voice agreement. *Our plan.*

*Our plan,* Tally echoed, sounding pleased with the word.

The tunnel marked Main Living Quarters opened into an old wooden house built right into the rock. There was a carved wooden lintel over the door, and shuttered windows on either side. The door itself stood slightly ajar.

The air inside smelled faintly of cedar and long-dried flowers. The wood floorboards had been worn down into shallow hollows, where a man had walked the same paths for years and years. The house had the feeling of a place whose owner had stepped out for just a moment and never returned.

As Maureen crossed the threshold, something cold crawled up her spine. Every hair on her arms stood on end, as if a razor-sharp blade rested just behind her neck. Then the sigil in her palm glowed with a clear light like moonlight, and the cold vanished from her body, as if an invisible hand had lifted itself away.

Maureen’s body finally caught up with what had happened. The sweat on the nape of her neck turned cold, and her pulse began thudding in her throat like a drum. Her fingers felt stiff and thick. She remained perfectly still, taking three long breaths and one short one in Silver Light before she continued moving.

The hall was simply furnished: writing desk, high-backed chair, some shelves. A fat book lay on the desk, its cover black shot through with red veins. A huge painting hung on the wall, dominating the centre of the hall. It showed a giant serpent rearing up to the sky, hissing, its black scales catching the lamplight. The artist’s brush had been very fine when it came to the scales: when Maureen’s shadow made the light shiver, it looked as though the scales themselves moved, slithering over each other in a deadly dance. She found herself looking at everything but the painting’s eyes, in the same way she would avert her gaze if she met a stranger in a dark lane.

“Heir. You’ve come.”

The voice came from the chair. As Maureen spun to face it, a pile of motes of light resolved itself into the shape of someone seated. They gathered on the seat, drifting down like sand poured from the top of a sack, until the outline of a human form was complete. Stooped, thin as smoke, features that slid away whenever you tried to look at them directly. It was him. Gregory.

The figure rose from the chair and spoke, looking past Maureen’s shoulder, addressing someone who wasn’t there.

“So many years. So many. Someone has finally woken this old thing up.”

“You read the lines, heir,” Gregory said. “No one does that by luck, so you have a real gift. But—” He paused, looking off into space, as if he were staring at some door that wasn’t there. “This road. The Ancient Wizards’ road. Are you sure that’s where you mean to walk?”

Maureen realised she had answered him. In her head, yes, to a man who no longer had ears to hear. Her throat worked once around nothing.

“Ancient Wizards.” The figure sighed, settling back into the chair. “Ah. Ancient Wizards…” Then it brightened slightly. “Well. Go a long way down the road to power, heir. A long way.”

The figure began to break up, like sand blown apart by the wind. Its pieces drifted down to settle on the seat of the chair, leaving an empty room behind.

When the recording had said ‘heir’, Tally had not said anything. Now it asked, almost hesitantly, *Would you like me to file this with his other lines?*

*Yes,* Maureen thought silently. *Yes. All of them.*

She thought about Adrian, his eyes never leaving her face as he told her he wasn’t in the habit of selling his own apprentices. She thought about Jing Sheng’s notebooks lying unread on a white lab bench, where nobody would ever look at them again.

She said nothing. Not aloud, and not to Tally.

The sigil flared on her palm, growing hot enough that she felt as if she’d laid it against an open flame. It lifted away from her skin and hung in the air, displaying itself as a huge silver circle on the stone floor beneath her feet. After a moment the image flickered once and then was gone.
