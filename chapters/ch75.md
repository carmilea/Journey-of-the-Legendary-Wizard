The herb tea Adrian had poured her sat forgotten in her hands, now cold from sitting too long as he talked. A burner ticked away on the long stone bench beside him, the black flame dancing around the test tube it held without giving off a single degree of heat. Somewhere on the other side of the Dark Forest’s edge, Gregory’s residence still held a meditation method and the essence blood of the Child of Purgatory. She’d come back with every intention of being on her way there in just a few days’ time. Now that plan slipped out of her, like a breath she couldn’t take back.

“Every apprentice,” Adrian said again, not raising his voice in the least. “Ours, theirs, the Bone Tower’s, the things from underground. One realm, and the order is to kill.” He paused for a moment, letting that sink in. “And we go in thinner than any of them.”

A cold sensation ran down the backs of her arms. She let her shoulders sag just a bit so Adrian could see a frightened apprentice. It was only partially an act. “Mentor, this trial—” she began, then stopped. There were no appeals here. They both knew that.

Adrian didn’t look at her. Instead he rested a fingertip on the black flame beneath the test tube. It required no adjustment. “Unexpected for everyone, Olsen. The Forest will give you something before you go in. It’s not stingy when it wants something.”

*What kind of something?* Tally asked. *I have no data on that. Promise logged.*

*Promises from the Forest get their own column,* she told it silently.

“The ones who walk out,” Adrian continued, sounding like a tired merchant reading off a list of goods he didn’t believe in. There were long pauses between each item, as if he were considering whether or not to include it. “Purple Stone Flower. Flame-Tail Hawk feathers. Silver-Trace Ink. Things I’d have to haggle for myself.”

At the last item Maureen’s thumb froze where it rested against the rim of her cup. She made sure her breathing didn’t change, didn’t speed up or slow down in the least. Her eyes remained fixed on the cold dregs at the bottom of her cup. Her thumb was the only part of her that moved.

[Silver-Trace Ink: 1 of 4. Source: trial.]

Tally ticked the totem column into the corner of her vision. It had no data on the Purple Stone Flower or the feathers, but it still logged them.

*I’m getting it,* she said silently.

*Then we should plan for getting it instead of surviving,* Tally said. *Those are two different plans.*

*Both,* she said.

Tally sighed. *Both columns, then.*

*Yes.*

Adrian took her for afraid, and he tried to comfort her, though it wasn’t his trade. “Don’t . . . It’s dangerous. It’s also a door. Live, kill enough of theirs, and even an advanced meditation method could be . . .” He trailed off, then continued. “The families never let those out. Never.”

She could see how much the effort cost him. After what he’d told her about junk methods and false paths, Maureen could see the hook beneath the sweet words. Adrian couldn’t hide that easily. His gaze didn’t leave the flame.

Maureen smiled just a little. “Mentor. Not every apprentice is going, are they?”

For the first time since he began speaking, he looked directly at her. “Fair, Olsen.” He said the word as if he were tasting a spoiled reagent. “The old families were here before these walls. Whatever fair is, they wrote it.”

Tally translated: *The strongest apprentices from the families stay home, meaning there will be fewer strong opponents in the realm. Grim, but better odds.*

*You’re so cheerful.*

*I’m precise,* Tally said.

A small, hard smile pulled at one corner of her mouth. She had already known the answer before she asked, but confirmation never hurt. The most gifted children would remain behind their family walls, but no one was going to ask for a reason. No one needed one.

“Winnie’s asked after you twice this week,” Adrian said gruffly.

“Tell her I’m back,” Maureen said.

“Tell her yourself. Eat something first. You look like the road.”

Maureen bowed her head, and by the time she reached the door, Adrian was focused on his bench once again.

The black-stone corridor bustled with wizards moving in pairs down its length, and cold radiated from the walls. Gregory’s door was a few days’ ride away, and now she’d been told that she was heading in the other direction. Maureen counted what she carried: grenades, potions, a Seal that was almost dark. Then she turned toward the dormitories.

* * *

The dormitory district was more crowded than she remembered, with apprentices called back from all over. A door slammed somewhere up ahead, and Maureen caught the faint sound of someone swearing before they clapped their hand over their mouth. The air smelled of lamp oil and too many people breathing it. Faces passed by, set in grim lines, and not one of them turned in her direction.

The dull brass key turned as smoothly as ever, and she was alone at last.

“Back again,” she said to the empty room.

Everything was exactly as she left it. The dust-removal spell had done its work perfectly, leaving no trace of time passed since she’d left. In the study, the faint greenish ring where she had first set her Recovery Potion still stained the surface of the stone workbench. She touched it with a fingertip, feeling the coolness of the stone against her skin. This bench remembered her better than anyone else in this castle did.

Nothing had changed since Tally’s last frame of the room, it told her. Not by a thread. Tally even sounded a bit offended that the cleaning spell had better filing habits than it did.

*Jealous of a cleaning spell?* Maureen thought at it.

*Of its filing system,* Tally responded.

She ran the bath water hot, then climbed into the tub. The water tasted slightly of iron pipe. Grime from the road and dust from the realm floated away from her skin, and a knot between her shoulder blades released for the first time since the realm. She hadn’t even been aware of it before.

She slept straight through the night, no bell cord stretched across her door and no crossbow under her hand. When she woke up, the lights had come on by themselves and her head felt clear. Maureen lay in bed for a moment, staring up at the ceiling.

She’d been putting this off since she left the road. Now that she was awake and rested, she had no further excuse for delay. Maureen snapped her fingers once—her old habit when starting a task—and asked for spirit and totem. Tally responded with the information as though it were a herald unrolling a scroll before her eyes.

[Spirit: 7.4.] [Minai Totem: 4%.]

Maureen read the 7.4 twice. A flush of heat crept up the back of her neck. It had been 7.8 when she walked out of the realm.

*You sure you didn’t drop a decimal?* she asked it silently.

*I did not,* Tally said. *You spent it. Seventy-one shells charged by hand with Ancient Andas fire-charging, each one paid for out of your spirit. Fatigue . . . if you rest, I expect you back to approximately 7.8.*

Maureen lay in bed for a while longer, letting the 7.4 dance in front of her eyes.

*So I’ve been paying myself out like stones,* she thought.

*You have,* Tally replied.

*At a worse rate than Gandolf ever gave you.*

She couldn’t get back what she’d already used. She would have to find some way to increase her store. Spirit grew the same way that water carved stone—with patience and time. She remembered the grey-haired apprentices that haunted the Gathering, still climbing at a hundred and fifty, and her jaw set. She had searched for anything that might raise it. She hadn’t found much.

The rest of Tally’s report was waiting for her.

[Rune lines: understood.]

[Totem analysis: complete.]

*Both finished on the road,* Tally said. *That four percent is the lines you’ve already laid within your body. You need the rest of them, and that takes things we don’t have.*

*Ink,* Maureen said silently, letting the word fall on its own line.

*Ink first,* Tally agreed.

A headache began at her temple. Two numbers and four missing materials. The warmth of sleep drained from her body, as if someone had opened a window in the room.

*I saw guards as I came in,* she said silently to Tally. *Is that right?*

*You were correct. There are twice as many on the outer corridors as I recorded the last time. Each and every one faces inward.*

*Inward,* Maureen thought. *Not out.*

*So . . . the trial isn’t optional?*

*It never was.*

“I’m getting that ink,” Maureen said to the empty room.

To live long enough to get it, she needed more trump cards. The alchemical grenades were the best she had. One trigger word and they went off. You could never have too many of those. Tally ran a count on the Hillan Metal she had left over from Albert’s small stock and what she had purchased during her travels.

*Enough for a handful of shells,* it said.

*Then we buy,* Maureen said.

*Then we pay,* Tally responded. *Easy to find, painful to afford.*

Maureen climbed out of bed and washed her face. She dressed, settled the Cross Sword at her hip, then headed toward the Trading District.

* * *

The arcade looked different this time. Tables full of war goods had pushed out the herb merchants—racks of crossbows, plates of armor resting on cloth, a grinding wheel shrieking as someone worked on his blade. The place smelled like a camp the night before a march.

*War is good business,* Tally said. *Prices are up by about a third on everything I have on file.*

*Someone always makes money,* Maureen replied silently.

Gandolf’s shop looked the same: the same stoppered jars, the same single lamp that hissed when it burned. A thin film of dust lay across the top of the stool behind the counter. The labels on the jars out front were written in a cramped hand Maureen did not recognise.

Maureen felt a prickle run across her scalp.

*The wick on the hanging lamp appears untrimmed,* Tally said. *In every frame I have of this establishment, Gandolf trimmed the wick before you reached the counter.*

*I see that,* Maureen replied silently. Her neck grew tight.

She rapped on the counter with her knuckles. “Gandolf.”

Nothing answered from the back room.

“Gandolf!” she said again. Her voice sounded too loud in the narrow space.

“Who’s yelling?” called a young man’s voice, and a purple-haired youth shuffled into view from the back room. His hair was a wild mop, his face covered with freckles, and there was a yellowish crust in the corners of his eyes. He scowled like a man who’d been roused from sleep and intended to make someone pay for it.

*Two purple heads on file,* Tally noted. *This one bears no resemblance to Sawin in any other respect. You should probably not shoot this one.*

“Where’s Gandolf?” Maureen asked. “This is his—”

“I know, love,” he said, cutting her off with a lazy grin. “The old man? Gone. Dead. You’re a bit late. Shop’s mine now.”

His words hit her like a missed step in the dark. She kept her hand flat on the counter, feeling beneath her palm the hollow worn into the wood by years of an old man’s elbows.

“How?”

He shrugged. “Depends on who you ask. Some say he couldn’t let it go, had to be a real wizard before he croaked. Went down the Underworld with a crew, digging for something to get him over the top, and someone got him down there instead. Others say he locked himself in here to break through and just . . .” He puffed out his cheeks and then let them deflate slowly. “Pop. Who knows?”

Tally filed both stories as hearsay and added two columns to hold them. *One old man, two deaths,* Tally said. *No data on which one he got.*

The purple-haired youth suddenly snapped awake and his eyes narrowed into slits. “Wait. You knew his name. You’re not after the shop, are you? He owed me stones, see. Lots. It’s mine. Fair and square.”

*Convenient,* Tally noted, filing the debt under Unverified.

*Yes,* Maureen agreed silently. A debt no one else could verify was the easiest kind to inherit.

“I bought here,” she said. “For two years. I brew.”

His face flipped over like a coin. “Brew? An apothecary? Well, well! For you, discounts across the board. The finest stock in the arcade. My word on it.”

She gave him the look one might give a stain, then walked out of the shop while he was still talking. His smile hung in the lamplight behind her like a forgotten rag.

Out in the arcade, Tally spoke more quietly. *Moving Gandolf’s sample to deceased,* it said. *He was my first Third-Class,* it added. *He’d want you to charge for this.*

*He would,* Maureen told it silently. Maureen remembered him haggling over half a stone as though it were drawn blood. She remembered the expression on his face when he’d broken their deal: I lost a counter, not my word. For a moment the muscles in her throat clenched. Then she turned and began making her way toward the Potion Apprentices’ Gathering and George’s stall.

* * *

She had walked but a few steps when raised voices sounded down the corridor. The echoes off the high, vaulted ceiling made them stumble and break awkwardly. The words reached her in bits, broken and stumbling: “No—I didn’t—I never—” followed by “That’s a lie! You’re making it up—”

Tally matched one of the voices before she made the corner. *Richard, by voice,* it said. She had already increased her pace.

As she rounded the corner, Maureen saw a ring of dark robes had formed. It was not a tight formation, but a loose and comfortable one, the way people form a ring when they are watching a dogfight. The gathered apprentices were giving off a warm hum of conversation and the smell of sweat mixed with the scent of hot lamp oil. One of them laughed, smothering the sound behind a hand. No one moved forward. In the centre of the ring, Richard’s face was red to the tips of his ears as he shouted up at the large apprentice. The big man stood a head taller than anyone else nearby. Lucy was pressed against Richard’s back, both small fists buried in his robe’s sleeve, her eyes swimming with tears.

Richard’s voice cracked and quivered, just as it had when he’d been fourteen. His hands shook at his sides, but he hadn’t moved from his position in front of Lucy. Something in Maureen’s chest grew very still.

Tally analysed the big apprentice, calibrating his physical traits against what it knew. Second-Class. Upper-middle column. Knight body: Lower–Middle. *Bigger than he is strong,* Tally added.

The big apprentice threw his arms wide, addressing the crowd. “Hear that? The duke’s pup can’t remember his debts. Stones you owe me, lordling, and now you’ve cracked my enchanted piece. Cracked! Know how much enchanted costs? Come on, let’s do sums—”

“Frank, that’s rubbish!” Richard jabbed a finger up at him. “I never touched your . . . whatever. You came here looking for a fight!”

*Nothing in the big man’s hands,* Tally said. *Nothing on the floor. Pouch empty. Flat against leg.*

*A setup,* she told Tally silently.

Lucy gave a wet little sob. The apprentices leaned forward, straining to see. The murmuring rose, like water in a pan coming to the boil.

The grin vanished from Frank’s face.

“Won’t own it? Fine. I’ll learn you.”

His fist clenched, and Maureen felt the first threads of wizard energy creeping up his forearm, slow and thick.

> Right fist. Energy in the forearm.

> Catch. No spell. No kill.

Tally’s words were cold and brief.

Frank surged forward. The huge fist drove straight toward the middle of Richard’s brow, sending a gust of air ahead of it.

It stopped halfway. Maureen Olsen’s hand had snapped up to close around it, and the blow died in her palm with the suddenness of a punch meeting a solid post.

Frank blinked down at her, a slight girl in a dark robe. The big man’s knuckles ground in her palm, the wizard energy in his forearm prickling against her particle sense like heat from a stove.

Her expression gave nothing away. Her hand had moved before Tally had even finished issuing instructions.

“What—” Frank shook his head, trying to pull his fist free. “Who’re you? Little girl wants to put hands on me? You want to die? Do you know who I—”

*He was about to tell her,* Tally said.

She didn’t let him.

Maureen tightened her grip. His bones began to give beneath her thumb, one after another, like green sticks snapping, and his knuckles began folding back the wrong way. His sentence became a scream.

She felt gritty pieces of broken bone through his skin. He was big, and heavy, and loud. What Maureen felt, though, was not the weight or the shattering sensation or the screams. It was something cold and attentive and neat.

While he was still screaming, her other hand found his throat. She felt his pulse jump beneath her fingers as she lifted the big man off the flagstones, his boots kicking at empty air. The crowd of onlooking apprentices took a single, collective breath, and the whispering died out completely. Behind Richard, Lucy’s half-sobbed word of fear ended abruptly.

Maureen brought Frank down on the stone floor, the dull impact shuddering up through the soles of her boots. The big man managed to get his eyes open, looked up at Maureen standing over him, then let out a yell of pure fright. Maureen kicked him in the head. Frank lifted off the ground, slammed into the bare stone wall beside them, and slid down its rough surface, limp, with his eyes rolled up in their sockets. His chest still moved with shallow breaths. He didn’t move otherwise.

*No spell cast,* Tally said, its voice warm once more. *Mana untouched. He lives, and all witnesses saw him strike first. Physical brawls are tolerated in the Forest, as in the case of Jax at the Potion Apprentices’ Gathering. Spells and fatalities are not. Tidy.*

*Log it as a brawl,* Maureen thought back.

She lowered her foot slowly, the only movement in the corridor. In the silent corridor, the lamps’ flames were suddenly loud with their hissing. Somewhere at the back of the crowd, someone took a careful step away from her.

Richard stared at her with his mouth half-open, his finger still frozen in the air, pointing at nothing.

Lucy, both fists knotted in the back of Richard’s sleeve, stared at her too. She seemed to be looking at Maureen for the first time.
