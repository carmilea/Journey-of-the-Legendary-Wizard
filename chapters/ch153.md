It had been a long climb through tunnels that wept cold water like sweat. Denise’s body hung limp over her shoulder, as light as a sack of wet wool would be for a knight’s augmented body. Her breath was warm against the back of Maureen’s neck, faint and shallow with the rhythm of sleep.

[Ascent: day 3. Denise: pulse 72. Load: 1 person, your knight body. Fine.]

*I didn’t ask,* Maureen responded silently.

*I know,* Tally answered. *I was going to tell you anyway.*

At each stop to rest, Maureen tipped a few drops of recovery draught between Denise’s lips and dusted the scrape on her temple with hemostatic powder, feeling the bitter grit of the unabsorbed powder on her own fingertips when she was done.

The light around them slowly became thinner, shifting from lamp yellow to brown to grey before they felt the first breeze of surface air against their faces. The temperature drop made her skin sting, and she could smell the sharp resin of pine trees and the clean scent of snow. When she looked down at her feet she could see frost sparkling on the stones as her boots crushed them.

They reached the staging camp, and the mortal men and women who had waited with the wagons for the column’s return began to rise from their blankets. Forty-odd hired wizards had gone down that passage with Claude and Jackman. Two figures emerged from the passage and one of them was being carried.

[Camp: 14 mortal, alive.]

 *They’ll repeat everything Denise says. Every one of them will say it over and over, so it had better be right.*

*Then we make sure it’s right before Denise wakes up,* Maureen responded silently.

“We will not be returning with the remainder of our column,” Maureen said in Richard’s low and flat voice. “Make ready Denise’s carriage and team. The wagons will follow.” None of the grooms or drivers argued the point. Maureen took Denise’s own carriage and team, and laid the unconscious girl on the couch at the back. She pulled the quilt up around Denise’s chin with two careful fingers, then climbed up onto the driver’s bench. Fourteen pale faces watched the carriage roll away into the frost.

* * *

The axle creaked as the carriage wheels rolled over frozen ruts. Maureen Olsen sat hunched on the driver’s bench, her hood pulled down to her eyes, both hands clamped around the reins so tightly that she felt the knuckles ache. She wasn’t going to mention it, not even to herself, but there was a dull headache pounding behind her eyes. Before Denise stirred from her slumber Tally replayed the Oserroba heir’s pulse from the platform. *She went under before the serpent came. The last thing she saw was the wizard Richard’s face falling over the edge . . . Edward’s face. And after that . . . nothing.*

Maureen began constructing the lie silently, line by line, while the horned beasts paced along the road. The drugged potions. Waking up late. The devastation in the cavern. The Night Mother Eve and the purple heart gone. Tally checked off each point against what Denise had actually seen, and against the fall of Edward. *It is consistent,* Tally assured her. But consistent did not mean proof. Denise might not believe it.

Behind her there was a creak from the couch. A rustling sound, and then a gasp, as Denise tried to raise herself on an elbow, almost toppling over sideways. Maureen heard the quick intake of breath, the hiss of air between clenched teeth. *Body has no strength left. No wizard energy either.*

Without turning around, Maureen spoke. “Denise. You’re awake.”

“Wizard Richard?” The answer came back too quickly, half a gasp. There were no tells of doubt or disbelief in the girl’s voice. *Not proof,* Tally reminded her. *But she knew your voice before she saw anything at all.* *The voice held,* Tally noted.

“We’re . . . alive?” Denise asked, as if testing the word on her tongue.

“We are,” Maureen said. “Both of us.”

Maureen continued in an even voice, as though she were some shop clerk reading off a receipt of goods sold. “The drugged wine took out most of them, but I’m an apothecary. I never go anywhere without something up my sleeve.” So she’d never gone fully under.

“By the time I could stand again, the place was a shambles, dark elves everywhere, dead and dying. The Night Mother was gone. The purple heart was gone. No one was paying attention to us, so I picked you up and walked out.”

Denise didn’t speak. For the next few moments Maureen listened to the sound of the wheels and the sound of the cold air, working its way into the carriage despite her hood, and thought about silence. Silence was a thing that could be measured as well as heard. This was the silence of someone fitting pieces together, testing each piece before it was put in place.

“And Claude? The others?”

Maureen didn’t sugarcoat it. “Dead. Claude, Jackman, all the other wizards who went down there with him. Everyone but you and me.”

Through the open panel behind the driver’s bench, Maureen could hear Denise’s breath stop, then start again unevenly. No sob followed, just the creak of the quilt as fingers closed upon it.

“Rest,” Maureen said. “Don’t think yet.” She tossed a tube of pale purple Official-grade recovery draught back over her shoulder, letting it land on the quilt instead of turning around to hand it to Denise.

[Recovery stock: 3.] *As I warned you, this is a low supply.*

*I know,* Maureen told her silently. She needed it.

*Yes, that is kind,* Tally replied. *Kindness log noted.*

Maureen caught the glimpse of Denise’s knuckles whitening around the glass tube out of the edge of her eye as the frost-white fields slid past the window. Her own eyes remained on the road.

* * *

The night air was filled with the spitting of the fire, as it cooked up droplets of pine resin from the wood they burned. A fine layer of frost coated the grass, making it glitter silver in the dying light of the fire. The horned beasts shifted underneath their blankets. Maureen sat with her hood up on a fallen log, her robe wrapped tightly around her.

The heart of the Void Mirage within her shifted, and Maureen felt it.

Not-Tally voices murmured underneath the crackling of the flames. A high-pitched buzzing echoed in her ears. The world shifted slightly, sliding a finger’s width to one side.

[Void interference: voices, by your report. Eyes: glow, dark-red + violet.]

*Those are not my voices,* Tally said firmly. *Count with me. One two three four. Pause. One two three four.*

With Maureen’s consent, Tally reached through the Darklight Orb to steady her. [Mana: 20.1 → 19.9] The display flickered along one edge where the heart of the Void Mirage crowded against the anchor of the Orb.

*The Void Mirage is affecting the Darklight Orb,* Tally said simply.

Before Maureen’s boots the frost sparkled with a faint light. Maureen could see it now, the light from her own eyes reflecting off the silvered grass. Patterns of dark red glowed against patterns of bright violet, twisting and turning within one another. Neither colour giving any ground.

There was a soft crunch of frost behind Maureen. “Wizard Richard,” Denise said. She had managed to get back on her feet without assistance for the first time, and had wrapped herself in a travelling cloak. Her voice was soft, almost a whisper.

As she spoke, the glowing patterns faded from the frost. Maureen turned her head, and when she did so, her eyes were just deep black beneath her hood once more.

*Three seconds,* Tally said. *If the Oserroba had spoken any faster, she would have witnessed the glow.*

“Thank you, Richard,” she said, bowing deeply. “For the draught. For carrying me out.”

Then she straightened up, studying the hood.

“Is something wrong?”

“No.” Maureen moved further along the log. “Sit, if you’re cold.”

[Void Mirage heart: held.]

[Absorbed: est. 9%.]

[Full refinement: months. Low confidence.]

*Peak Fifth-Tier, by Gregory’s entry, no sample. It’s only a matter of time,* Maureen thought.

*Time is one thing that my estimate is honest about not knowing,* Tally said.

The first few days out of the deep had been terrible. Hallucinations had plagued her every waking moment, the inside of her skull had felt split down the middle, her thoughts viscous as tar. This night the fire was real, the heat warming her face as she sat. That was progress.

They talked for a little while. Denise asked how far it was to Conta.

“Four days, if the frost holds,” Maureen replied, and neither of them said Claude’s name.

Denise’s hand drifted to her sleeve for just a second, the old tell that she was worried. “Goodnight, then,” she said, standing up and walking back to the carriage. Her shadow stretched long across the silvered grass.

At dawn, Maureen knelt by a still stream to wash, and Tally caught the change in the water.

[Jaw drifting. Recast.]

The changes to her body since the swallow were pulling Richard’s face out of shape. She recast Mimicry.

[Mana: 20.1 → 19.7]

In the still water, edged with ice, Richard’s mousy features and weak jawline settled back into place over her face, the black eyes peering out from beneath the hood. She tugged the hood forward, then stood up.

* * *

The carriage rolled through the gates. The steward, who had been waiting on the step to greet them, froze at the sight of both Denise and Wizard Richard. “Lady Denise. Wizard Richard. I…” He turned and bellowed into the house.

Tally tracked his bows. Four. The same as the last time they came home. Some habits could survive anything.

Maureen washed, ate and slept in the guest room, behind a small ward. Tally kept watch and reported in the morning.

[Night: 3 passes, servants. Ward drew 0.1. Mana: full.]

From her window, she watched the black crepe knotted to the gate, stiff with frost, and saw the camp wagons creaking up the drive. Tally logged them with the fourteen.

By noon, everyone knew. Claude, Jackman, and every wizard they had taken were dead. Only Lady Denise and Wizard Richard returned whole.

The mourning in the corridors was quiet and swift. The servants bowed ever so slightly lower to Denise than they had a week ago, while Claude’s old men stood in tight knots that fell silent when Richard passed. Denise and Claude had been a hair’s breadth from open war, Tally noted, and with Claude gone, the family was Denise’s.

*The succession fell into Denise’s lap,* Maureen thought.

*Pushed. Both columns.* Tally corrected.

The steward found Maureen in the gallery, standing in front of the dark oil portraits. “The master wishes to see you, and Lady Denise, together.”

* * *

Outside Lawrence’s sickroom, Maureen laid two fingers on the steward’s sleeve. When she touched her collar, he was to call the young mistress out for some urgent matter, then bring her back and stand quietly outside this door.

The steward bowed his head and Maureen felt his pulse jump.

Tally overlaid the room onto Maureen’s sight, rendering it with pale blue lines. The door was left half-open by design, and it was positioned at an angle to the bed that faced toward the window. If Denise stood there, Tally said, she would be able to hear every word spoken, without being seen by the bedridden man.

Inside, the low winter sun poured a rectangle of light through the tall glass windows onto the white silk sheets. The air lay warm and heavy with the scent of old camphor and bitter sickroom herbs. Lawrence lay perfectly still beneath the coverlet. His grey hair was combed flat against his head. His eyes were sunken into their sockets but bright as a bird’s.

“You’re back.” Lawrence’s voice sounded thin through his dry throat, but it was warm. “Days without a word. I was beginning to think . . .” His gaze lost focus, staring up at the ceiling. “Claude. My brother.”

[Lawrence: breath 7/min. Pulse 84.]

*Grief is evident,* Tally reported. *Not provable, but logged.*

Denise knelt beside the bed, her thumb counting her father’s knuckles beneath the sheet. She laid a layer of soft words over her dislike for Claude, like a cloth placed over a stain.

“I didn’t bring the Spring back, Father,” she said. “I’m sorry.”

Lawrence patted her hand. “Hush, child. The Spring is unimportant. You’ve come home. That is all I wanted.”

Tally flagged the pouch. A bath’s worth of the Spring’s water sat within.

*There is no data that it will heal him,* Maureen said silently.

*There is also no data that it will not,* Tally replied.

Maureen said nothing, and Tally logged the silence as a conscious decision.

Denise rubbed her father’s shoulders. Her face softened, smoothed by years, becoming young and girlish again. Maureen watched the warmth in Denise’s expression and kept her own hands still at her sides.

Maureen touched her collar, and the steward appeared immediately with an urgent matter in the east wing. Denise rose from her father’s bedside and followed him out.

“Wizard Richard.” Lawrence turned his head on the pillow. It was the only part of him that moved. “You walked beside my daughter into that place. The Oserroba family will not forget it, and our thanks will not be small.”

The footsteps faded down the corridor. They stopped, then returned and stopped again. A shadow fell across the bar of sunlight lying on the floor just inside the doorway. Tally identified the density at the door: Denise, close enough to touch.

Maureen looked at the man who could not move.

“You’d have paid anything for that water,” she said. “Even her.”

Lawrence’s eyebrows drew together. Puzzled, but polite, he did not otherwise react. His body remained motionless beneath the white silk sheets. “I don’t follow you, Wizard Richard.”

Tally cued up the relevant data. The day of the jest. The 0.8 second pause before the word “anything” when asked what he would trade for health. The two separate occasions where Lawrence began to speak to Richard at his own bedside, then stopped. Tally reminded Maureen of what the vault really contained: legend and song, no methodology. She reminded Maureen of the amber flags upon Claude’s branch.

*Bluff on the gap,* she advised. *Not on a page we don’t have.*

“Claude’s tale fooled Denise,” Maureen said. “But it couldn’t fool the man who keeps the keys to the vault. I’ve read every record in it.”

She continued laying out what was missing. Songs, legends, one small scrap of text about white water flowing beneath the dark. No route, no methodology. So the patriarch knew from the beginning that there was nothing behind the promised cure.

Then the amber pages upon Claude’s branch. The decade during which the ledgers changed hands.

Lawrence did not move. When he spoke again it was with his own breath. “Thousands of documents, Wizard Richard. Unsorted. The Spring is just a few lines of song lost in all that text. How could you possibly—”

He saw her half-smile. He did not finish the sentence.

[Lawrence: 84 → 121.]

Tally translated the tells: first fear, then calculation.

Lawrence closed his eyes. It was a long time before they opened again. When they did so, they were hard. “Yes. I knew his story was hollow.”

The sickbed seemed to fall away from him.

For a moment, the head of a great wizard house stared out through those sunken eyes. The warm air pressed around Maureen like a hand. “It changes nothing now,” Lawrence said, his voice low and level, “and telling it gains you nothing. Name your price, Richard. I still live. I am still head of this family. There is much I can give you. A very great deal.”

*He is bargaining with you,* Tally said quietly. *You can stop now.*

*Not yet.*

Maureen thought of Duke Skran, setting a heavy purse beside her plate.

She leaned forward, bending close over the bed. The force of Lawrence’s gaze did not touch her. “You told me once you’d give anything to be healed. I didn’t believe you then. I do now.”

She patted the man’s shoulder, as gently as any nurse might. Then she straightened and stepped out of the sunlight.

In the doorway, she brushed past Denise. Tears ran down the girl’s face. She was backing out into the corridor, step by stumbling step. Maureen did not look at her.

Behind Maureen, Lawrence’s voice cracked out once. “Denise!”

The sound of running footsteps faded in the other direction down the dark corridor.

[Denise: pulse 140, running.]

Lawrence’s cry echoed back from the stone walls, unanswered by human voice. Faintly through the half-open door came his voice: “I’m sorry, Denise. I’m sorry.”

The corridor was empty and silent save for the sound of Maureen’s footfalls. Tally spoke quietly in her head. *That was cruel, what you did to him.*

*It was true,* Maureen replied silently.

*Both columns.* Tally sighed. Then, softer: *Your jaw just unclenched. First time today.*
