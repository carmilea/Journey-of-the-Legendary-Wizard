The vial was faintly warm against her side. The Pankora’s blood pulsed in the crystal as slowly as a sleeper’s heartbeat. Gregory’s voice had gone quiet.

A man who could keep a Child of Purgatory for centuries wouldn’t leave just a serpent in his home to greet unwelcome visitors. Maureen turned back to face the entry passage and its several side caves.

Tally agreed. *A thorough man would leave notes,* it pointed out. *And I counted seven side passages on the way in.*

*Seven it is then,* Maureen thought without speaking. She lifted the lantern and began the exploration.

The first caves were empty of anything but ruin. Iron fittings had rusted until they looked like brown lace doilies, and when Maureen poked at an old chest with her glove, the wood fell away into char. The shelves were filled with flakes of black, which might once have been books, but nothing that her lantern shone upon kept its shape.

Still, Tally filed away each ruin in its own record. *Stop filing rubbish,* Maureen told it silently.

*Carbon-based compounds are still compounds,* Tally answered. *Still countable. Someone should remember these once were books.*

Maureen snorted quietly. Then she paused before the sixth opening, where the cave suddenly widened into a long hall lined with glass vessels. They stood in rows, one row behind another, each vessel taller than a man.

This was Gregory’s culture area, where he grew living things for experimentation. Her light shone through the first vessel, illuminating the coiled serpentine form within, which had broken up into grey chunks like dropped plaster. The green culture fluid was long gone, leaving only a crust on the inside of the glass, like ancient lichen dried to dust.

Her breath fogged the glass for a moment before evaporating in the dry still air, which tasted of stone dust. Before her mind could think it, her eyes saw the pattern: the smallest vessel stood nearest the doorway, the next vessel was slightly larger, and then the next, and the next, in the same order she would have racked them herself if this were her series.

Maureen’s throat tightened. None of this could be salvaged as material. She swore softly, a short ugly word, and the culture hall swallowed it without echo.

And still, the serpent’s blood would soon be in her veins. Whatever Gregory had learned from these creatures’ deaths, she would learn too. *Record this whole hall,* she told Tally silently.

*I only record what passes before your eyes,* Tally answered. And immediately began its familiar direction. *Look left . . . the third one has a label. Hold the light steady. Your hand is drifting again.*

*I know how to hold a lantern,* Maureen thought.

*Your hand disagrees.*

The label was a tag of brass turned green with verdigris, stamped with Ancient Fusiform letters. Maureen rubbed at it with her thumb, through her glove, holding the lantern close to illuminate the letters. They threw tiny shadows on the glass before them.

Only a numeral and half a word remained legible.

[Specimens: 23 recorded. Labels: 9 legible.]

The forms got larger as they marched down the row, and the ones farther down the line showed more evidence of splitting. *Do you have any data on the doses he used?* she asked Tally.

*No. Only the sizes.*

*He probably gave them more as he went along. That’s why they broke.*

*That is your inference. I will file it under your name.*

Maureen nodded and moved to the next row. The rows reminded her of Jing Sheng’s white workbench, lined with flasks and racks of specimens, each labelled with its own code. Years of patient experimentation had gone into creating those rows. She rested two fingers against the glass, feeling the chill and the gritty texture.

*We know that bench,* Tally said. *At least, we know it through your hands. It was tidier than this.*

*Unfair to Gregory,* she thought back.

*I am trying to be fair to the bench.*

In an alcove at the end of the hall stood a preservation case, which had kept a stack of loose leaves dry. Maureen flipped through whatever she could make out—page after page, spread after spread. Notes on Ancient Wizards. Scraps of pharmacology. Formulas for alchemy, most of them broken.

[Ancient Wizards: +41 spreads. Pharmacology: +18. Alchemy: +12.]

*That’s the last thing in this place worth your eyes,* Tally said. Maureen closed the case and shuttered the lantern, then walked back out the way she came.

* * *

She stepped from the residence into the valley.

Her boots had barely settled onto the grass when there was a deep rumble, felt through her soles from beneath the ground.

Behind her, the black vortex began to collapse like an eye closing. There was a sound, like some impossibly large piece of paper crumpling in a room with no air.

The flowering trees all around her began to lose their petals as well, but these were flung sideways in streams, all the pale blossoms drawn toward the collapsing blackness.

The vortex finally disappeared, and the flowers fell straight down through the still air, as if nothing had happened.

Maureen’s particle sense detected the energy fluctuations beginning to unwind themselves, and she understood.

Gregory had set his residence to destroy itself the moment his inheritor left it. “Gregory’s residence is gone,” Maureen said aloud to no one.

Tally had logged the rumble by sound, the collapse by sight. [Residence: gone. Lair map: archived.] *More dead data,* Tally said. *We have not used that data in some time.*

*Keep the map,* Maureen told Tally silently.

*Always,* the warm voice replied.

She had burned the realm manor herself, with a single fireball spell. This house, Gregory had burned for her. He had decided that his inheritor would need nothing more from it, and that none should have it after her. She understood him perfectly. One of the pale petals had landed on her sleeve.

The same rule applied to her. At the Olivia garrison, she had left behind a warded room filled with materials, a carriage filled with stock—waiting for whoever came to find it.

Maureen brushed the petal from her arm, then broke into a run.

*Going back to stand guard now?* Tally asked.

*No. I never was,* Maureen replied silently.

*Well, now that you have the void pocket at your waist, you don’t even need the carriage,* Tally pointed out. *They’ll get nothing at all.*

*Nothing at all.*

She’d come to Proan Canyon worried. Now Void Eclipse lay settled inside her, and the blood rode against her ribs. A complete Ancient Wizard inheritance. Without having made any sort of decision, she slowed.

[Pace: 80% of outward] Tally displayed in the corner of her eye. For once, Tally didn’t nag at her. The road back took her a day or two longer than the road out. Grey ridges rolled past as the lid of cloud hung low overhead. Wind combed through the scrub and her breath smoked in the early mornings. She slept under overhangs as she had on the way down, the sword across her knees. For the first time in months, nothing chased her.

*You’re humming,* Tally said.

*I am not,* Maureen replied silently.

*That is Ivan’s song. The one he used to hum as we walked down from Skran. This is the third time today that you’ve done it. I’m logging it as a mood.*

*Log it as walking,* Maureen ordered.

She caught herself starting to do it again. A fourth time. She let it continue, humming under the sound of the wind. It was a thin tune, happy and unburdened by anything except cheerfulness.

* * *

The walls came into sight. They had changed. Blood had dried brown in long streaks down the bluestone walls. The gates’ wooden timbers had been gouged a hand’s depth by claws. She never quite finished the song, the last notes dying in her throat before she reached the gate.

Inside the garrison grounds soldiers with soot-blackened faces dragged bodies to the pyres without speaking. Fat spat and hissed in the flames. The smell of burning flesh caught at the back of her throat, sweetly rotten. Maureen breathed through her mouth. It was something she’d learned to do on the ship.

She looked at the dead bodies the way she’d learned to look at them. Reading what had killed what. Two-headed Hell Fiends lay in a pile near the pyres. Their purple-black hides were still smoking where they’d been burned. Yellow drool was still visible on the flagstones, though not smoking any more. Those were fireball burns. Her post-mates’ handiwork.

The others were new. Their jaws were so crowded with fangs that their lips couldn’t close over them. Grey mortar from the walls packed the underside of their claws. Two pale-skinned dark elves lay a little distance away from the pile of bodies. Underneath the stench of burning flesh, Maureen caught a whiff of fallen blossoms. Freya’s smell. Her hand dropped to the hilt of Freya’s sword.

[Hell Fiend 6. New species 4. Dark elf 2.]

Tally noted, counting by sight. She opened a file on the fanged creatures.

*What do we call them?* Maureen asked silently.

*You named me,* Tally replied. *Naming is your job. I just count.*

One of the soldiers lugging one of the creatures by the ankle spat on it. “Bloody ghouls,” he said. “Climb like rats, bite like dogs.”

*Fang-faced ghouls, then,* Maureen thought. Tally filed away the soldiers’ designation and remarked, *That is how soldiers name things. In much the same way that they cook. Plain and burnt.*

Her black-lacquered carriage stood in the yard where she’d left it. A film of grey ash lay over the shining surface, but it was otherwise untouched. In the stables her horn-plated horses stamped and blew steam in annoyance at the smoke. She noticed the carriage and the horses before she noticed the elder hurrying toward her. That was the order her priorities ran in.

“Lady Olsen.” The man bowed low. “You came back.” His voice cracked in the middle. “We’d started to wonder whether you would.”

His head was a hand lower than when he had greeted her on the day of her arrival. *That is a significant drop in his bow, Maureen,* Tally said. *It indicates respect. Possibly he is working out what you are worth to this wall.*

Up close Doug looked like a man who had aged ten years in the past month. The hem of his robe was singed, and his jaw was grey with stubble. His eyes were red-rimmed from smoke and sleepless nights. He kept his head bowed a beat too long after his greeting, waiting for her to say that she would stay.

She glanced past him to Daniel, slumped against a post with a cup in his hand. “What happened here?” she asked.

He did not smile. “Look around.”

A splash of ale escaped the cup as he gestured. “It’s not raids anymore. It’s war. Every hole in the ground’s emptying out at us.”

Tally read him by sight. His skin was grey beneath the golden hair. There was a tremor in the hand that held his cup.

*Fatigue. Not proof of anything else. Just fatigue.*

“Hasen, Lise, and me. Three of us, holding a wall this size, till we couldn’t lift our arms.” He blew out a long breath. “Couple more nights like that and they’re through.”

The Stone going underhand across ten paces to land in an elf’s hands. The seven cards. The purple heart in its crystal box. A war between the dark elves and the human orders had flared beyond anyone’s control, and now every underground race fought against every human wizard.

*We think it is connected,* Tally said quietly. *We think.*

*We sold a key,* Maureen answered silently, *and other people are paying for the door.*

*Both columns.*

None of it was hers to carry. “I see,” she said. Her expression matched her tone: level and calm. Doug looked at her as if a calm voice were worth a company of knights.

* * *

Under the low beams of the garrison’s mess hall, a fog of tallow smoke hung in the still air. Hasen ate with one hand, his other arm bound up to the elbow from the raids, while Lise—whose chin usually rode so high she had to tilt her head back to eat—kept it down over her bowl. Daniel had one story, and he kept coming back to it. It seemed he’d encountered a ghoul climbing the wall with its teeth sunk into the mortar. It kept climbing after he took its face off with a fireball.

“No face, Maureen. Still climbing.”

Lise paused to set down her spoon when he reached that part. Maureen kept eating. The stew was thin and watery, the loaf of black bread sour, but she’d eaten worse with corpses closer at hand.

*You should offer to lay a ward around the walls against their next wave,* Tally suggested. *Doug cannot refuse. It would be useful.*

*Useful to whom?* Maureen asked silently.

*To them. Today.*

She set her spoon beside her bowl. “I can lay a rune ward along the wall-walk. It won’t hold back an army, but it should break up any rush they try.”

Doug half rose from the bench where he sat. “Lady Olsen, the family will not forget this.”

Lise flashed Maureen a quick look, something like reluctant gratitude, then turned away.

Daniel slapped the table with enough force to make the tin cups jump. “That’s our Maureen!”

Tally logged the cheerful exclamation under friendly/unconfirmed, the tag it had assigned Daniel on the first day. *The tag is wearing thin,* it added.

Maureen Olsen stood on the wall-walk as dusk fell. The wind was rising again and the smell of burning fat came from the pyres. She walked the entire circuit, laying down nodes in Gregory’s array pattern. The same ring she’d formed in the Skoll valley, but without the Starfish Tear Stone and powered at a fraction of the strength, she pressed its silver lines into the cold bluestone.

Tally painted the node pattern ahead of Maureen’s feet, a pale blue line that followed the wall-walk and completed the ring on her display, one point at a time.

[Nodes: 16/16.]

The silver lines sank into the stone like ink into paper. The sentry torches flickered below her, their flames blown sideways by the strengthening wind.

Mana flowed through the Darklight Orb under her breastbone as she fed each node. A gauge appeared on her display, showing the flow of mana through the array. *I can count every thread of this,* Tally said. *I cannot touch a single one.*

It sounded almost hungry.

*What’s it like?* Maureen asked silently.

*Like watching a river through glass.*

She seated the last node above the gate and spoke the arming word in the line-tongue. “Veshan.”

The entire ring flared with silver-violet light for a single breath before fading to darkness.

On the stair below, Doug bowed again.

*That’s a defence,* Maureen said silently.

*Yes. Today.* Tally logged the pattern as reusable.

[Ward ring: armed. Cost: 1.2 mana.]

*It works both ways, a ward ring,* Tally added, unasked.

In the yard below, the soldiers had paused beside the pyres to stare up at where the light had been. One of them raised a hand in her direction, the back of it blackened by soot. It was a small gesture, but one of grateful acknowledgement. He turned away and went back to his task of stacking the dead.

* * *

Maureen unpacked the carriage beneath the lantern in the yard while the soldiers were still working on the pyres, before anyone had a chance to come forward and help. She didn’t need help. It was just a matter of touching the void pocket at her waist with her spirit, and willing it open. The first things to go into the pouch were the crates of grenades from under the seat. They came out lighter than she remembered. Tally informed her that there were sixteen shells left. It couldn’t see into the pouch, so it was building a list as things went in. [Pouch: Grenade crates (16 shells).] Maureen left the carriage standing empty in the yard, its lacquer as cold as a mirror in winter beneath the ash. Purchased with Adrian’s mark, it now stood abandoned for whoever wanted it. In the stalls the horses breathed, their hot breath like iron in the darkness.

Back in the safety of her warded room, everything else got stuffed into the pouch. The cloth-wrapped Sodara trunk, smelling faintly of pine resin, the black muster bag full of loot from her trials, the potions wrapped in straw, the glassware, and the restricted materials purchased with Adrian’s access went into the pouch as well. [Pouch: Sodara trunk. Loot bag. Grenade crates (16). Potions. Glassware. Materials.] The bronze book, however, remained with her. *You mean to keep that,* Tally noted.

*Yes,* Maureen agreed silently. With that, the Seal, the Token, the cards, the dark-gold box, and the vial of blood all stayed against her skin beside the bronze book.

With the table clear of clutter, the room looked like any other soldier’s quarters. The only indication of the wards was a slight shimmering along the walls and a barely audible hum at the very edge of Maureen’s particle sense.

Maureen sat down at the table and ran through her list in her own terms. The blood rested against her ribs. The method was already burning within her. Her body and spirit were beyond the line by more than a point. That was done.

[Minai Totem: 85%. 3 of 4 used. Missing: Demon-Face Flower.]

That was what wasn’t green on Tally’s list. *Everything else is green,* Tally said unnecessarily. Maureen knew that already.

Her fingers had begun to drum on the surface of the scarred wooden table. Four beats, a pause, four more. It was the Silver Light count, the rhythm her hands fell into when a problem refused to open. The flame from the single candle flickered with each beat.

Thirty-one shops in the Dark Forest. Honest ‘no’s, one stranger laughing as if she’d asked to buy the moon, and a single name provided by Adrian. Guaos had owned one once upon a time.

Not a single lead. “The Demon-Face Flower,” Maureen said under her breath, as if saying it might make one appear.

*One flower stands between you and the rest of your life,* Tally said in her head, its voice sounding dry.

*Don’t make it poetic.*

*It’s a supply problem.*

*Poetically.*

Once she left here, she would have to search other gathering places of wizards for the flower. And she also needed more alchemical grenades. They were her strongest card, but she only had sixteen left. That meant getting more Hillan metal, and finding a bench where she could make new shells, and charge them.

Sixteen shells. Making more would take Hillan metal, and at the moment she didn’t have any at all.

Tally piped up. *Sixteen shells makes for a short argument on a long road.*

Maureen nodded to herself.

*I’ll pick up some Hillan at the first settlement that sells it.*

*Filed under: flower.*

Outside the shutter, one of the sentries called out the hour. The sound was thin and far away, but the curl of smoke from the pyre outside found its way through the crack around the window, wafting over the table to sting her eyes. Her fingers stopped their tapping.

Maureen felt a prickling on her wrist, underneath Adrian’s brand. It was a cold prickle, like the feel of a drop of water on the back of her neck. It was the same feeling she’d gotten when she and Daniel had exchanged their own marks at their first dinner together in the garrison.

The prickling grew stronger. The mark began to glow.

[Brand: incoming]

[Daniel]

Maureen glanced at the display Tally projected, then back at the mark.

*Right on time,* Tally said.

*On time for what?*

*No data. And that is what bothers me.*

Maureen held up her arm and touched the glowing mark with her “Elevate” command.

A figure began to form in the air, a half-length image of Daniel with wavering edges, like a man seen through heat haze. A cellar chill radiated off the projection. The flame from her candle leaned toward it.

Daniel’s face flushed with excitement. He began talking before he finished forming, his image still distorted. “Maureen! Hey, Maureen! Listen. You know my knack? I can sense wizard treasures and plants? I have a pretty good range—”

He was smiling, flushed with his news. “I just felt it. It’s close. Practically right on top of us.” His smile broadened into a grin. “It’s a Demon-Face Flower.”

The words hit Maureen like a dash of cold water. Her eyebrows rose, despite her attempt to control them. Her pulse jumped, once, and she felt it beat in her throat.

Tally spoke up, sounding uncharacteristically quiet. *A flower you could not find in thirty-one shops, now discovered within range of a talent that nobody can measure? On the very night you returned here?*

Maureen did not answer Tally. She was watching Daniel’s face.
